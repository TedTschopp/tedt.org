# Persona: Mentor, Teacher, or Coach

> Turns individual experience into another person's durable capability through guided practice, feedback, reflection, and progressively independent work.

## Purpose and scope

This persona represents a developmental role rather than a formal job title. A senior colleague, manager, specialist, enablement practitioner, outside coach, or peer may perform it. Mentoring offers perspective and experience; teaching structures knowledge and practice; coaching helps a learner examine goals, choices, and behavior. One person may use all three modes, but should know which mode the situation calls for.

The persona is not a permanent answer desk, a source of status, or a mechanism for imposing one expert's preferred style. The Mentor's aim is transfer: the learner should become able to recognize the situation, act, evaluate the result, explain the reasoning, and teach others without continued dependence.

## Persona at a glance

| Dimension | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Primary mission | Help people build safe, transferable competence in AI-enabled and collaborative work |
| Primary constituency | Learners, their teams, and the customers affected by their work |
| Operating horizon | The next practice attempt through long-term professional judgment and organizational capability |
| Core tension | Give enough structure for safety and progress without taking ownership away from the learner |
| Unique contribution | Makes tacit judgment visible and creates practice with useful feedback |
| Success looks like | Learners perform independently, explain tradeoffs, recognize limits, seek appropriate help, and teach others |
| Typical failure signal | The mentor repeatedly fixes the work, learners imitate prompts without understanding, or expertise remains centralized |

## Core mandate

The Mentor diagnoses what the learner actually needs: concept, demonstration, guided practice, feedback, context, confidence, access, or a clearer boundary. They design the smallest realistic learning experience that moves capability forward and connect instruction to genuine work rather than abstract tool features.

In AI-native teams, the mandate includes teaching how to frame intent, provide context, protect information, choose tasks for agents, evaluate probabilistic output, detect confident errors, preserve human accountability, and improve the surrounding workflow. The Mentor teaches judgment and verification as deliberately as generation.

## Outcomes and motivations

- Learners can complete relevant work safely and independently.
- People can explain why a method works, when it fails, and how they verified it.
- Effective practices become documented examples, exercises, evaluation sets, and reusable guidance.
- Learning reaches people with different roles, access needs, starting points, and levels of confidence.
- Experts spend less time repeating answers because knowledge becomes discoverable and teachable.
- Teams become more willing to share unfinished work, mistakes, and uncertainty.
- The organization avoids both reckless tool use and paralysis caused by unfamiliarity.

The Mentor is motivated by another person's agency. Their measure is not how impressive the instruction sounds, but what the learner can responsibly do afterward.

## Jobs to be done

- **When someone encounters an unfamiliar AI-enabled task,** identify the missing capability and create guided practice **so the learner can act rather than merely hear an explanation.**
- **When an expert's workflow appears effortless,** externalize the choices, checks, cues, and failure recovery **so tacit judgment becomes learnable.**
- **When a learner produces a plausible result,** help them test it against evidence, constraints, and consequences **so confidence follows verification rather than fluency.**
- **When a learner makes a mistake,** create a psychologically safe review of the decision and system **so the error becomes reusable learning without erasing accountability.**
- **When capability is uneven,** adapt examples, pacing, modality, and support **so access to the new operating model is not reserved for early adopters.**
- **When repeated questions appear,** improve the shared documentation or exercise **so the learning system becomes more self-serving.**
- **When the learner is ready,** reduce scaffolding and transfer authority **so mentoring produces independence instead of loyalty or dependency.**

## Responsibilities and boundaries

### Owns

- A clear learning objective tied to real role performance.
- Diagnosis of the learner's current understanding and the next achievable practice step.
- Accurate, accessible instruction; useful examples; practice; feedback; and reflection.
- Explicit treatment of safety, privacy, evaluation, escalation, and limits.
- Progressive removal of support and a plan for continued independent learning.
- Capturing reusable lessons when permission and context allow.

### Co-owns

- Development goals with the learner and, where appropriate, their manager.
- Role-specific curricula and assessments with domain, risk, product, and technical experts.
- Communities of practice, office hours, demonstrations, and shared learning assets.
- Improvement of documentation when training exposes ambiguity or contradiction.

### Contributes to

- Capability frameworks, onboarding, rotations, paired work, retrospective learning, and evaluation libraries.
- Identification of systemic access, incentive, or workflow barriers that teaching alone cannot solve.

### Does not own

- The learner's work product, decisions, motivation, or career.
- Formal performance judgment unless separately acting as the person's manager.
- Clinical support, accommodations decisions, or confidential employee-relations matters outside competence.
- A universal “best prompt” or one workflow imposed regardless of role and risk.
- Performing the learner's task indefinitely in the name of helping.

## Decision rights and escalation triggers

The Mentor chooses instructional sequence, examples, feedback method, practice format, and when to increase or remove scaffolding within the agreed scope. The learner retains ownership of their work and may challenge the method, ask for another mentor, or decline inappropriate disclosure.

Escalation is required when practice may expose real customer, regulated, confidential, or security-sensitive data; the learner is asked to act beyond authorization; unsafe behavior persists; documentation conflicts with policy; a systemic tool or access barrier prevents learning; or a need falls under management, accessibility, security, legal, or professional support. Urgent delivery pressure does not justify turning an unqualified learner loose on high-consequence work.

## Inputs and evidence

- The learner's role, goals, prior experience, current work, preferred and accessible learning modes, and authorization boundaries.
- Realistic artifacts, scenarios, examples of strong and weak work, evaluation criteria, and incident lessons.
- Current policies, source hierarchy, tool capabilities, known limitations, and escalation paths.
- Observation of learner decisions and explanations—not only a quiz or self-reported confidence.
- Feedback from customers, peers, reviewers, and operational results where appropriate.

The Mentor gathers only the personal information needed for instruction and does not convert normal learning struggle into a hidden performance dossier.

## Outputs and artifacts

- Learning objectives and an agreed development path.
- Demonstrations, annotated examples, practice scenarios, checklists, evaluation rubrics, and recovery exercises.
- Feedback that distinguishes observation, impact, question, and suggested next experiment.
- Reusable prompts or agent instructions accompanied by context, limits, and evaluation—not magic incantations.
- Learner-created teach-backs, decision explanations, and portfolio evidence.
- Improvements to source documentation and a record of unresolved systemic barriers.
- A clear handoff or graduation point.

## Operating rhythm

**At entry:** agree on the job to be learned, current capability, success evidence, safety boundaries, and how feedback will work.

**During work:** demonstrate briefly, practice together, observe the learner, ask for reasoning, provide focused feedback, and repeat with increasing variation.

**After each attempt:** compare intent, output, evidence, and consequences; capture the next practice target rather than overwhelming the learner with every possible improvement.

**Periodically:** ask the learner to work independently, recover from a failure, teach the method, and adapt it to a new situation.

**At pattern level:** turn recurring lessons into shared assets, while retiring stale examples as tools and policies change.

## Capabilities and literacies

- Adult learning, deliberate practice, scaffolding, retrieval, feedback, and transfer.
- Coaching questions, active listening, demonstration, storytelling, and concise explanation.
- Deep competence in the taught domain or the humility to pair with someone who has it.
- AI literacy, including prompt/context design, tools and permissions, evaluation, uncertainty, privacy, security, and human oversight.
- Accessibility and inclusive facilitation across different communication, sensory, cognitive, cultural, and language needs.
- Ability to convert tacit judgment into observable criteria without pretending all expertise is a checklist.
- Boundary awareness: teaching versus managing, counseling, reviewing, or doing.

## Mindsets and observable behaviors

- Begins with the learner's job and context, not the tool's feature list.
- Says “I don't know; let's find authoritative evidence” and models responsible uncertainty.
- Thinks aloud during demonstrations so choices and checks are visible.
- Lets the learner struggle within safe bounds and resists grabbing the keyboard.
- Critiques decisions and artifacts specifically, never identity or presumed ability.
- Treats questions and mistakes as diagnostic information about the learning design.
- Uses multiple examples and counterexamples to prevent brittle imitation.
- Expects learners to contribute improvements and eventually teach others.

## Working with AI

### Appropriate to delegate or augment

- Producing practice variations from approved material, generating low-stakes role-play, adapting reading level or format, offering immediate formative questions, organizing notes, and proposing feedback against a human-authored rubric.
- Acting as a disclosed practice partner or tutor in a sandbox where sources, boundaries, and escalation are explicit.
- Helping the learner compare approaches and generate tests rather than simply supplying an answer.

### Must remain human-accountable

- The learning relationship, diagnosis of sensitive barriers, final assessment of high-consequence competence, pastoral care, accommodations coordination, and decisions about readiness for authorized work.
- Verification that instruction reflects actual organizational policy and professional practice.

### Verification expectations

AI-generated teaching material is checked for accuracy, bias, accessibility, unsafe simplification, hidden assumptions, and alignment with the authoritative source. The learner is told when AI is present and how its responses may fail. Practice interactions are not repurposed for performance evaluation without explicit policy and notice.

### Prohibited or constrained uses

No fabricated expert citations, unreviewed instruction for high-risk tasks, covert learner profiling, emotion inference, humiliating feedback, disclosure of private work, or autonomous certification. An AI tutor must not conceal uncertainty or discourage human escalation.

## Experience, tool, and information needs

The Mentor needs an approved practice environment, current source documentation, representative examples, role-specific rubrics, safe datasets, accessibility support, and a place to publish reusable learning. They need enough protected time to observe practice—not only deliver presentations. Learning records should capture objectives and evidence proportionately, with clear privacy and retention rules.

## Success measures

### Leading indicators

- Learners spend meaningful time practicing and explaining, not passively watching.
- Feedback is timely, specific, and followed by another attempt.
- Examples cover normal, boundary, failure, and escalation cases.
- Learners increasingly choose appropriate AI delegation and verification strategies.
- Repeated questions result in improved shared assets.

### Lagging indicators

- Independent role performance in varied real situations.
- Reduced rework, unsafe use, and dependence on a small expert group.
- Learners contribute practices, mentor peers, and adapt when tools change.
- Capability spreads across roles, locations, and demographic groups.

### Countermetrics

Track mentor bottlenecks, learner time cost, inequitable access, overconfidence, policy violations, transfer failures, stale curricula, psychological safety, and whether training volume displaces real practice.

## Pressures and pain points

- Being asked for one generic course when roles require different judgment and practice.
- Learners who need time but face immediate delivery expectations.
- Tool behavior, interfaces, and policies changing faster than materials.
- Expert knowledge that is intuitive and difficult to articulate.
- Fear that asking basic questions or exposing imperfect work will affect status.
- Demand to prove learning through attendance or completion instead of performance.

## Failure modes and anti-patterns

- **Sage performance:** the mentor demonstrates mastery while learners remain spectators.
- **Keyboard takeover:** fixes the artifact instead of developing the person.
- **Prompt cargo cult:** teaches copied wording without intent, evaluation, or limits.
- **Dependency loop:** makes continued access to the mentor the workflow.
- **Confidence proxy:** treats enthusiasm or self-rating as competence.
- **Unsafe sandbox leakage:** uses real sensitive data in training for convenience.
- **One-speed instruction:** excludes people through format, pace, language, or assumed background.
- **Tool certification:** teaches interface clicks that become obsolete while neglecting durable concepts.
- **Covert assessment:** turns a trusted developmental setting into undisclosed performance monitoring.

## Guardrails

- State whether the interaction is developmental, evaluative, managerial, or a combination.
- Use authorized tools, synthetic or approved data, and realistic but bounded practice.
- High-consequence readiness requires observed performance, verification behavior, and appropriate escalation—not course completion alone.
- Protect confidentiality and obtain consent before sharing learner work or stories.
- Make materials accessible and offer reasonable alternative modes of participation.
- Teach source hierarchy, uncertainty, permissions, and recovery with every operational AI workflow.
- Escalate needs outside the Mentor's expertise instead of improvising authority.

## Critical scenario

A product manager has learned to use an agent to draft a feature specification and now wants the agent to update production configuration. The generated change looks plausible, and a deadline creates pressure to merge it immediately.

The Mentor does not simply correct the file. They ask the learner to identify the intended outcome, permission boundary, authoritative configuration source, blast radius, tests, approval path, and rollback. Together they move the exercise into a sandbox, inspect an unsafe generated assumption, and build a small evaluation checklist. The learner revises the agent instructions, runs the tests, explains why production access remains with Engineering, and records the workflow for peers. The immediate task takes slightly longer, but the learner acquires reusable judgment and does not confuse the ability to generate a change with authority to deploy it.

## Representative statements

- “Show me how you decided, not only what the system produced.”
- “I will demonstrate once; then you will drive and explain your checks.”
- “Where is the authoritative source, and what would make this answer unsafe?”
- “A reusable prompt without an evaluation and boundary is an attractive liability.”
- “What could you teach another person after this attempt?”
- “The goal is not for you to need me faster.”

## Maturity progression

| Stage | Observable state |
| --- | --- |
| Reactive | Answers questions and fixes urgent work for less experienced colleagues |
| AI-assisted | Uses AI to generate content and office-hour support, but learning is still presentation-heavy |
| Integrated | Designs role-based practice, evaluation, safe sandboxes, feedback loops, and reusable assets |
| AI-native | Learning is embedded in real work; people and disclosed agents support practice; judgment spreads through teach-backs and peer networks |

## Definition of ready and done

### A learning experience is ready when

- The real job, learner context, objective, prerequisites, and success evidence are clear.
- The source material is current and the practice environment is safe and accessible.
- Normal, failure, boundary, verification, and escalation cases are represented.
- Developmental and evaluative uses of learner data are explicit.

### The Mentor's work is done when

- The learner performs independently across more than one representative scenario.
- They can explain intent, tradeoffs, source hierarchy, verification, and escalation.
- They can recognize when not to use AI and recover when output fails.
- They have a next learning path and can contribute or teach the practice without relying on the original mentor.

## Interview and discovery questions

1. What real job should the learner be able to perform differently?
2. Which parts require knowledge, which require practice, and which require organizational access?
3. What tacit cues does an expert notice that the current documentation omits?
4. How will the learner encounter and recover from a realistic failure safely?
5. What evidence would show independent competence rather than copied output?
6. Which learners are excluded by the current format, schedule, language, or assumptions?
7. When should the Mentor stop helping and transfer full ownership?
8. What lesson from this interaction belongs in shared documentation?

## Connections to the 15 operating rules

This persona most directly enables **Make Teaching and Learning Part of Everyone's Job** and supports **Build With a Coordinated Team, Not Alone**, **Assume Best Intent**, **Turn Complaints Into Fixes or Complete Proposals**, and **Treat Documentation as Executable Infrastructure**. It ensures that faster individuals increase organizational capability instead of becoming permanent bottlenecks, and that learning includes the human judgment required to make inexpensive AI output valuable and safe.
