# Persona: Decision Owner

> Converts ambiguity into an accountable choice, records why it was made, and remains answerable for what happens next.

## Purpose and scope

The Decision Owner is the single accountable role for a defined decision. They clarify the question, determine the decision method and deadline, gather proportionate evidence, hear relevant challenge, choose, record the rationale, assign action, and revisit the choice when named signals change. Their presence prevents consensus theater, committee diffusion, and teams waiting for authority that no one has claimed.

This is an organizational role, not necessarily a senior title. Decision ownership should sit with the person closest to the consequences who has sufficient context and formally delegated authority. One person owns a decision even when many people advise, approve controls, or execute it. Ownership can transfer, but only explicitly, with scope and accountability recorded.

## Persona at a glance

| Dimension | Definition |
|---|---|
| Primary objective | Make timely, legitimate, evidence-informed decisions that lead to action and learning |
| Unit of attention | A precise choice, its consequences, rationale, owner, and review conditions |
| Optimizes for | Decision quality relative to stakes, speed, reversibility, and cost of delay |
| Protects against | Unowned ambiguity, endless consensus, hidden vetoes, stale choices, and lost rationale |
| Key partners | People affected, domain experts, product, engineering, risk, finance, process, and documentation owners |
| Typical time horizon | Framing through execution, outcome review, and supersession |
| Essential stance | Listen broadly; decide clearly; remain accountable |

## Core mandate

For each consequential choice, answer: What exactly is being decided? Who owns it? By when? Under what authority and constraints? Is it reversible? What evidence is enough? Who must be consulted, and who only needs to be informed? What action follows? What signal would trigger reconsideration?

The role scales deliberation to consequence. A reversible experiment should not wait for executive certainty. An irreversible, high-cost, rights-affecting, or safety-critical choice deserves broader evidence and challenge. The owner does not seek perfect certainty; they make uncertainty explicit and choose when the expected value of more information no longer exceeds the cost of delay.

## Outcomes and motivations

- Teams know who decides, what remains open, and when the answer is due.
- Decisions occur at the lowest competent level within explicit guardrails.
- Reversible choices become fast experiments; hard-to-reverse choices receive deeper review.
- Rationale, assumptions, alternatives, dissent, and constraints remain discoverable.
- Every decision produces an owner, next action, and observable result.
- Outcomes update future judgment rather than being used only to reward hindsight.

## Jobs to be done

- **When a team is circling an issue,** I want to state the decision in one sentence and name its owner and deadline, **so that discussion becomes purposeful.**
- **When evidence is incomplete,** I want to assess stakes, reversibility, cost of delay, and value of more information, **so that rigor is proportionate.**
- **When experts disagree,** I want assumptions and tradeoffs surfaced without forcing artificial consensus, **so that I can decide with dissent visible.**
- **When I choose,** I want to record rationale, conditions, and rejected alternatives, **so that execution is coherent and later review is fair.**
- **When implementation begins,** I want one accountable action owner and success signal, **so that the decision changes reality.**
- **When new evidence crosses a threshold,** I want the decision reopened without stigma, **so that flexibility is treated as disciplined learning.**

## Responsibilities and boundaries

### Owns

- Decision framing, scope, authority check, method, timeline, and final choice.
- Proportionality of evidence and consultation relative to stakes.
- Rationale record, including assumptions, constraints, alternatives, dissent, and review triggers.
- Ensuring a named owner accepts the next action and outcome measurement.

### Co-owns

- Evidence quality with product, research, engineering, finance, domain, evaluation, and risk specialists.
- Implementation plan and reversibility with the execution owner.
- Communication and durable record with the documentation owner.

### Contributes to

- Portfolio prioritization, architecture, process design, risk treatment, incidents, and learning reviews.
- Clarifying other decisions that depend on or constrain their own.

### Does not own

- Every task that follows the decision.
- Expertise they do not possess; specialists remain accountable for their evidence and interpretations.
- Authority beyond their delegation. They cannot accept someone else’s legal, security, safety, financial, or ethical risk.
- Unanimity. Consultation is not the transfer of accountability to a group.

## Decision rights and escalation triggers

The owner may select among options within delegated scope, set the decision deadline, ask for necessary evidence, stop discussion when the decision standard is met, choose a reversible trial, and reopen or supersede their own decision. They must clearly distinguish decision, recommendation, approval, consultation, and execution.

Escalate when authority is missing or disputed; the choice crosses budget, policy, legal, security, safety, labor, customer-rights, or risk-appetite boundaries; two decision owners have overlapping mandates; consequences extend materially beyond the owner’s scope; required evidence is unavailable; conflicts of interest impair legitimacy; an irreversible choice could create severe harm; or an exception requires acceptance by a different risk owner. Escalation should present the decision, options, evidence, deadline, and precise authority requested.

## Inputs and evidence

Inputs include the customer or organizational outcome, constraints, live portfolio context, affected parties, options, opportunity cost, cost of delay, reversibility, technical and operational feasibility, experiment and evaluation results, financial analysis, security and legal interpretations, process impact, customer research, dissent, precedent, dependencies, and explicit unknowns.

The owner distinguishes observed fact, model output, forecast, expert judgment, preference, and assumption. Evidence need not be exhaustive, but it must be decision-relevant and its provenance visible.

## Outputs and artifacts

- One-sentence decision statement and explicit owner.
- Decision brief with context, outcome, deadline, authority, options, and evaluation criteria.
- Reversibility and consequence assessment.
- Consultation map: decides, advises, executes, is affected, and is informed.
- Decision record with choice, date, rationale, evidence, assumptions, dissent, constraints, and rejected alternatives.
- Action assignment with accountable owner, next step, success signal, monitoring, and due date.
- Review trigger, expiry where relevant, and supersession record.

## Operating rhythm

At intake, the owner distinguishes a decision from an information or work request, then classifies consequence and reversibility, identifies advisers, and sets a deadline. Context moves asynchronously. Any meeting focuses on unresolved tradeoffs and ends with a decision, owner, and next step. The record publishes promptly. At a trigger or review date, the owner compares outcomes with assumptions and affirms, adjusts, reverses, or retires the choice.

## Capabilities and literacies

- Framing, systems thinking, critical reasoning, and probabilistic judgment.
- Reversibility, optionality, cost of delay, expected value, and value of information.
- Interpretation of customer, technical, financial, operational, quality, and risk evidence.
- Facilitation of dissent and pre-mortems without surrendering accountability.
- Clear rationale and governance literacy across authority, approval, risk acceptance, and separation of duties.

## Mindsets and observable behaviors

The effective owner is decisive without pretending certainty. They ask who bears the consequence, seek disconfirming evidence, and state what would change their mind. They use neither rank nor consensus to avoid accountability, move quickly on reversible choices, slow for one-way doors, credit dissent, and change course when triggers fire.

## Working with AI

### Delegate

AI may summarize evidence, identify missing stakeholders, generate alternatives, compare criteria, model scenarios, find precedents, draft pre-mortems, and prepare the record.

### Retain as human accountability

Humans frame the consequential question, establish values and criteria, assess legitimacy and human impact, resolve value conflicts, make the choice, accept risk within authority, communicate it, and remain accountable for follow-through.

### Verify

Trace claims, test assumptions, seek disconfirming evidence, inspect omitted options and groups, validate calculations, and route specialist conclusions for qualified review.

### Prohibited or constrained

AI may not be recorded as the accountable owner, accept residual risk, grant its own authority, conceal dissent, fabricate rationale, or make high-impact decisions without the required human process. Confidential deliberation and personal data use only approved systems. Automated reversible decisions still need human-defined boundaries, monitoring, appeal, and stop conditions.

## Experience, tool, and information needs

The owner needs clear delegation, strategy context, working artifacts, decision and risk registers, current customer and operational evidence, scenario tools, secure collaboration, versioned records, dependencies, and trigger dashboards. They need access to affected people and a norm that only evidence—not lobbying—reopens a legitimate choice.

## Success measures

### Leading indicators

- Percentage of material decisions with one owner, deadline, authority, criteria, and reversibility classification.
- Time from decision need to recorded choice, segmented by consequence tier.
- Consultation completeness and evidence provenance.
- Percentage of decisions with action owner, success signal, and review trigger.

### Lagging indicators

- Decisions that produce the intended outcome or useful learning.
- Rework caused by ambiguity, hidden dependencies, or lost rationale.
- Time from trigger to appropriate affirmation, adjustment, or reversal.
- Stakeholder understanding of what was decided and why.

### Countermetrics

- Fast decisions achieved by excluding affected people or bypassing mandatory expertise.
- High “success” created by never documenting failed assumptions.
- Number of decisions or meetings; volume is not quality.
- Stability achieved by refusing to revisit choices after material evidence changes.

## Pressures and pain points

Noisy evidence, urgency, lobbying, ambiguous authority, and fear of error encourage false consensus. AI can manufacture endless plausible analysis and make delay look rigorous. Invisible dependencies and hindsight distort judgment. The owner needs courage to close and humility to reopen.

## Failure modes and anti-patterns

- Asking a committee to decide without naming one accountable owner.
- Calling a recommendation or discussion a decision.
- Seeking perfect information for a reversible experiment.
- Rushing an irreversible choice because a date was announced.
- Recording only the choice and losing rationale, dissent, assumptions, and scope.
- Allowing the loudest or highest-ranking adviser to become an undeclared owner.
- Making a decision without an action owner or observable outcome.
- Reopening settled decisions through private lobbying rather than new evidence.
- Treating course correction as failure and hindsight as foresight.

## Guardrails

One material decision has one owner. Authority and risk boundaries are checked first. Consequence, reversibility, delay cost, and information value determine rigor. Consult affected groups and required specialists; disclose conflicts. Records capture rationale, evidence, assumptions, dissent, action, and triggers. High-impact choices preserve appeal and separation of duties; reversible choices include limits and rollback. Superseded decisions remain linked.

## Critical scenario: release now or wait for certainty

A team must decide whether to release an AI support assistant. Evaluation improves, but one customer segment has sparse evidence. The owner frames the choice as a bounded release—not universal approval—confirms authority, consults required experts, and makes uncertainty explicit.

The limited cohort excludes the under-evidenced workflow, requires citations and confirmation for account changes, and has quality and rollback thresholds. An operations owner runs it. The record explains why full release and full delay were rejected. When one language crosses its error threshold, that slice pauses without relitigating the product. Clear ownership made speed compatible with accountability.

## Representative statements

- “What is the exact decision, and who has authority to make it?”
- “Is this a one-way door, or can we learn through a bounded trial?”
- “I do not need everyone to agree; I need the strongest evidence and dissent visible.”
- “Here is the choice, why we made it, who acts next, and what would reopen it.”
- “New evidence changed the conditions. Revising the decision is responsible, not inconsistent.”

## Maturity progression

| Stage | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Reactive | Decisions emerge from meetings, rank, or urgency; ownership and rationale are unclear |
| AI-assisted | AI prepares options and summaries; humans still rely on inconsistent decision practice |
| Integrated | Ownership, tiers, records, action, and review triggers are embedded in work |
| AI-native | Routine reversible choices operate within machine-enforced bounds; humans focus on values, novelty, severe consequence, and accountable exceptions |

## Definition of ready and done

**Ready to decide:** the decision is stated in one sentence; the owner and authority are confirmed; deadline, consequence, reversibility, affected parties, options, constraints, criteria, evidence, unknowns, and required consultations are visible; and the cost of more information is understood.

**Done:** the choice is explicit and within authority; rationale, evidence, assumptions, dissent, scope, and rejected alternatives are recorded; required approvals or risk acceptance exist; a named owner has accepted the next action; success, monitoring, rollback, and review triggers are defined; affected parties are informed; and dependent records are updated.

## Interview and discovery questions

1. What exactly must be decided, by whom, and by when?
2. Who bears the benefit, cost, and risk of this choice?
3. Is the choice reversible, and what would reversal actually require?
4. What evidence could change the preferred option before the deadline?
5. Who must advise, approve, execute, be affected, or merely be informed?
6. What is the cost of delay versus the value of more information?
7. Which assumption or signal should trigger review?
8. Can a new team member reconstruct why the last decision was reasonable at the time?

2. **Cap Meetings at 60 Minutes:** ensures every decision meeting ends with decision, owner, and next step.

4. **Put Product in the Working Environment:** bases decisions on actual behavior rather than translated status.
5. **Do Not Let Product Control Engineering Time:** clarifies product, engineering, and decision accountabilities.
6. **Create a Daily Product–Engineering Jam:** makes small decisions while they remain inexpensive.
7. **Eliminate Unearned Monthly Meetings:** convenes only when interaction or a decision is genuinely needed.
8. **Stay Flexible in Pursuit of Value:** uses reversibility and review triggers to adapt responsibly.
9. **Assume Best Intent:** invites clarification and critiques evidence, not motive.
10. **Turn Complaints Into Fixes or Proposals:** demands problem, evidence, impact, proposal, owner, and action.
11. **Treat Documentation as Executable Infrastructure:** records authority, rationale, scope, and supersession.
12. **Expand Design Beyond Screens:** decides about permissions, errors, explanations, agents, and recovery.
13. **Organize Around One Exceptional Customer Experience:** makes customer outcome a primary criterion.
14. **Build With a Coordinated Team:** consults diverse judgment without diffusing ownership.
15. **Make Teaching and Learning Everyone’s Job:** reviews outcomes and shares reusable decision patterns.
