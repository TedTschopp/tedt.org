# Persona: Meeting Facilitator

> Protects human attention by convening only the interaction that is needed and ending every meeting with a decision, an owner, and a next step within 60 minutes.

## Purpose and scope

The Meeting Facilitator designs and guides synchronous collaboration toward a defined outcome. They decide with the meeting owner whether live interaction is warranted, move context and status to asynchronous channels, invite only necessary participants, structure productive challenge, hold the 60-minute boundary, and make closure explicit.

This is an organizational role, not necessarily a job title, manager, or neutral outsider. A team member may facilitate a daily jam; a trained facilitator may guide a high-stakes cross-functional decision. The facilitator owns the interaction process, not the substantive decision. The decision owner remains accountable for the choice, and action owners remain accountable for delivery.

## Persona at a glance

| Dimension | Definition |
|---|---|
| Primary objective | Produce a necessary collaborative outcome with the least synchronous attention |
| Unit of attention | A bounded interaction with explicit purpose, participants, inputs, and outputs |
| Optimizes for | Decision quality, participation, clarity, focus, and closure |
| Protects against | Status theater, agenda sprawl, hidden decisions, domination, and meeting dependence |
| Key partners | Meeting sponsor, decision owner, participants, documentation owner, and action owners |
| Typical time horizon | Async preparation, up to 60 live minutes, and durable follow-through |
| Essential stance | The meeting must earn the attention it consumes |

## Core mandate

Before scheduling, answer whether the work requires simultaneous human interaction. Information transfer belongs in a document, dashboard, recording, or asynchronous discussion. A meeting earns its place when real-time exchange materially improves a decision, creative synthesis, conflict resolution, sensitive conversation, relationship repair, or tightly coupled working session.

When a meeting is warranted, begin with one outcome and a known authority structure. End by minute 60—even if the issue is not fully resolved—with the decision or unresolved question, accountable owner, next action, due date, and written follow-up route. The limit is a design constraint that strengthens the organization’s written operating system, not an invitation to rush consequential judgment.

## Outcomes and motivations

- Calendars contain fewer meetings, and the remaining meetings produce observable value.
- Participants arrive informed because context is available and accessible in advance.
- The right people contribute without unnecessary attendees losing focus time.
- Disagreement becomes useful evidence rather than personal conflict or endless debate.
- Decisions, owners, next actions, dissent, and unresolved questions survive after the call.
- Work that does not fit the hour becomes explicit asynchronous follow-up or a smaller working session.

## Jobs to be done

- **When someone asks for a meeting,** I want to identify the exact outcome and test asynchronous alternatives, **so that live attention is used only when interaction adds value.**
- **When a meeting is necessary,** I want the decision owner, participants, prework, and method clear, **so that the hour is spent on the unresolved work.**
- **When discussion becomes broad or repetitive,** I want to restate the question, surface the tradeoff, and timebox the next move, **so that energy returns to the outcome.**
- **When power or communication styles distort participation,** I want structured contribution and visible dissent, **so that the owner receives the relevant judgment.**
- **When the 60-minute boundary arrives,** I want closure recorded, **so that unresolved work becomes owned rather than leaking into another hour.**
- **When meetings recur,** I want evidence of the decisions they produce, **so that unearned calendar commitments can be eliminated.**

## Responsibilities and boundaries

### Owns

- Meeting-or-async recommendation, outcome framing, agenda design, and timebox.
- Participation method, psychological and accessibility conditions, and discussion flow.
- Visible capture of decisions, owners, actions, parking-lot items, and unresolved questions.
- The 60-minute boundary and conversion of remaining work into an owned next format.

### Co-owns

- Participant list and prework with the sponsor and decision owner.
- Decision method and closure language with the decision owner.
- Durable notes, records, and distribution with the documentation owner or recorder.
- Meeting audit and team collaboration norms with operations and people leaders.

### Contributes to

- Conflict resolution, retrospectives, product–engineering jams, critiques, incident reviews, and learning sessions.
- Coaching people to write clear context and run smaller working sessions.

### Does not own

- The substantive choice, risk acceptance, technical answer, or action after the meeting.
- Forcing consensus when the designated owner must decide.
- Making an unnecessary meeting efficient. The correct intervention may be cancellation.
- Extending the meeting because preparation, authority, or evidence was missing.

## Decision rights and escalation triggers

The facilitator may decline or convert a meeting request when no synchronous outcome exists; require an outcome, owner, and minimum prework; recommend a smaller attendee set; interrupt repetition or harmful conduct; change the interaction method; call a break; close at 60 minutes; and publish that no decision was made if that is the truth.

Escalate when no legitimate decision owner exists; participants dispute authority; required legal, safety, security, labor, accessibility, or domain expertise is absent; undisclosed conflicts of interest emerge; behavior becomes threatening, discriminatory, or retaliatory; confidential information is exposed in the wrong forum; a high-consequence decision is being rushed without minimum evidence; or the sponsor demands false consensus or inaccurate notes. Escalation preserves safety and accountability, not merely schedule.

## Inputs and evidence

Inputs include a one-sentence desired outcome, decision statement if applicable, meeting owner and decision owner, authority and decision method, pre-read, options and tradeoffs, evidence and dissent, participant roles, accessibility and time-zone needs, confidentiality level, dependencies, prior decisions, and deadline. For recurring meetings, prior outputs and attendance patterns show whether the meeting earns its place.

The facilitator distinguishes context needed before the meeting from interaction needed during it. A pre-read should state what participants must read, think about, or contribute—not simply attach a large document without a question.

## Outputs and artifacts

- Meeting-or-async determination and rationale.
- Outcome statement, participant-role map, agenda, timebox, and participation method.
- Concise pre-read or links to authoritative context, sent with a response deadline.
- Live decision/action log visible to participants.
- Decision, owner, next step, due date, dissent, open question, and parking-lot record.
- Written follow-up, asynchronous review, or smaller working-session brief for unresolved work.
- Recurring-meeting scorecard: purpose, decisions produced, attendance cost, and renewal or cancellation date.

## Operating rhythm

Before scheduling, the facilitator tests documents, dashboards, recordings, polls, prototypes, and async threads against the outcome. If live work is necessary, they confirm authority, prework, and accessibility, canceling if readiness fails. The session starts with outcome, roles, method, and end time; interaction stays on the artifact or tradeoff. Minute 55 begins closure. Minute 60 ends the session. Notes publish promptly, unresolved work gets an owner and format, and recurrence must be renewed by evidence.

## Capabilities and literacies

- Outcome framing, agenda design, timeboxing, and group-process design.
- Listening, synthesis, neutral paraphrase, questioning, and de-escalation.
- Decision methods, authority mapping, consent versus consensus, and action assignment.
- Inclusive facilitation across hierarchy, culture, disability, language, location, and style.
- Product, engineering, critique, incident, risk, learning, async, and digital-collaboration patterns.

## Mindsets and observable behaviors

The effective facilitator respects attention as finite. They are warm but firm, curious about disagreement, and neutral about people—not process integrity. They name patterns without shaming, protect quieter voices, prevent rank from silently settling issues, separate dissent from veto, and record honest non-decisions rather than manufacture closure.

## Working with AI

### Delegate

AI may propose agendas, summarize pre-reads, find prior decisions, cluster input, transcribe with consent, track time, draft decisions and actions, and prepare accessible notes.

### Retain as human accountability

Humans decide whether to convene, design the social process, read power and emotion, protect safety, determine legitimate authority, interpret consequential nuance, confirm commitments, and own substantive decisions and actions.

### Verify

Confirm summaries against sources; have participants validate decisions and actions; check names, dates, dissent, omissions, and attribution; and apply retention and access rules.

### Prohibited or constrained

Do not use emotion or attention inference to rank people. Do not create covert recordings or transcripts. Do not let AI cast a deciding vote, assign commitment without human confirmation, infer consensus from silence, erase dissent, or publish confidential content outside its audience. Sensitive employee, legal, security, or customer discussions require approved tools and explicit handling.

## Experience, tool, and information needs

The facilitator needs shared artifacts, accessible conferencing, visible timers, structured input, decision and action logs, calendar analytics, async channels, recording controls, authoritative links, and role information. They need backing to cancel unready meetings, close on time, challenge unsafe dynamics, and accept async contribution in place of attendance.

## Success measures

### Leading indicators

- Percentage of meetings with one outcome, owner, prework, decision method, and 60-minute limit.
- Prework completion and ratio of necessary contributors to total attendees.
- Percentage ending with decision or explicit unresolved question, owner, next step, and due date.
- Percentage of meeting requests converted to successful async work.

### Lagging indicators

- Action completion, decision latency, and participant clarity after meetings.
- Reduction in recurring meeting hours, repeated debates, and decisions reopened for missing context.
- Participation quality and accessibility across roles and locations.
- Time returned to building, customer contact, deep work, and mentorship.

### Countermetrics

- Short meetings that produce poor decisions or move hidden labor to a few people.
- Fewer attendees achieved by excluding affected voices.
- High action count consisting of vague or low-value tasks.
- “Consensus” achieved through silence, hierarchy, fatigue, or unsafe dissent.

## Pressures and pain points

Sponsors schedule before defining outcomes, invite for political safety, and avoid written accountability. Participants arrive unprepared; hierarchy dominates; hybrid formats create unequal access. Facilitators are asked to compensate for missing authority or evidence. The 60-minute boundary feels impolite until unresolved work gains a better format.

## Failure modes and anti-patterns

- Treating every request as deserving a calendar event.
- Using an agenda of topics rather than outcomes and decisions.
- Spending the hour presenting material that could have been read or recorded.
- Inviting everyone “for visibility” instead of publishing a durable record.
- Letting status reports consume a working or decision session.
- Seeking unanimity when one accountable owner should choose.
- Allowing the loudest voice, rank, or fatigue to determine the answer.
- Reaching minute 60 with no owner or next step.
- Letting an AI summary replace participant confirmation of consequential decisions.
- Renewing recurring meetings without examining their outputs and attention cost.

## Guardrails

No meeting without an owner, one-sentence outcome, and reason for synchrony. Decision sessions identify owner and method. Prework is accessible and timely; attendance is role-based; others contribute asynchronously. Reserve five minutes for closure and stop at 60. Notes capture decisions and non-decisions, owners, actions, dates, dissent, and follow-up. Sensitive content follows consent and access controls; harmful conduct and false records are escalated.

## Critical scenario: the portfolio review that wants another hour

A portfolio review has 22 attendees, 40 slides, and two hours, while status already exists in a dashboard. Only one funding tradeoff is live. The facilitator cancels status, requests written exception updates, invites six advisers, and sends a two-page decision brief.

The session confirms constraints, compares options, surfaces customer and technical consequences, and tests dissent. At minute 55, the owner selects a bounded investment and names an experiment owner. One legal question becomes counsel’s written follow-up, due next day; funding is conditional on it. At minute 60, the session closes and the wider group receives the record. A recurring status meeting becomes focused decision work plus async visibility.

## Representative statements

- “What must be different when this meeting ends?”
- “Could a document, dashboard, recording, or async thread achieve this?”
- “Who owns the decision, and how will advice be used?”
- “We are discussing new context; does it change the stated tradeoff?”
- “We have five minutes: decision or open question, owner, next step, and due date.”
- “The hour is over. The remaining work moves to this written follow-up and these people.”

## Maturity progression

| Stage | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Reactive | Calendar-first coordination, status presentations, broad attendance, and unclear closure |
| AI-assisted | AI drafts agendas and notes, but meetings remain the default container for work |
| Integrated | Async-first norms, outcome-based agendas, decision ownership, inclusive methods, and 60-minute boundaries are standard |
| AI-native | Agents prepare and maintain routine context; humans convene sparingly for judgment, creation, conflict, trust, and accountable decisions |

## Definition of ready and done

**Ready to meet:** synchrony has a stated advantage; the outcome, owner, decision authority and method, required participants, prework, accessibility, confidentiality, agenda, and 60-minute limit are clear; participants have enough time to prepare; and missing evidence is explicitly accepted or the meeting is postponed.

**Done:** the session ended by minute 60; the intended outcome was achieved or the unresolved question is precise; decisions, dissent, owners, next steps, due dates, and definitions of done are confirmed; follow-up uses the smallest appropriate async or working format; notes are accurate and permissioned; and recurring need, if any, has an evidence-based renewal date.

## Interview and discovery questions

1. What interaction must happen live, and what can move to writing or a recording?
2. What exact outcome justifies the combined attention cost?
3. Who decides, who advises, who is affected, and who only needs the record?
4. What must participants know or contribute before the meeting?
5. Which power, access, language, or time-zone dynamics could distort participation?
6. What will happen at minute 60 if the issue remains unresolved?
7. How will decisions, dissent, actions, and follow-up become durable?
8. What evidence shows this recurring meeting still earns its place?
