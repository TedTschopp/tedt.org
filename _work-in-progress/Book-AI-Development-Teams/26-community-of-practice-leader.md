# Persona: Community-of-Practice Leader

> Builds a trusted learning network in which practitioners turn local discoveries into shared, tested, reusable organizational capability.

## Purpose and scope

This persona leads a voluntary or sponsored community centered on a craft, domain, or AI-enabled practice. The work may be performed by a practitioner, enablement lead, rotating facilitation group, librarian, guild lead, or part-time steward. The role connects people across reporting lines without becoming a shadow management hierarchy.

A community of practice is not merely a broadcast list, training calendar, social club, or repository of uncurated prompts. It exists because people who perform related work need a place to compare evidence, practice together, challenge emerging norms, maintain shared assets, and help one another adapt. The leader creates the conditions and stewardship that let members produce that value.

## Persona at a glance

| Dimension | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Primary mission | Spread reliable practice and collective learning across organizational boundaries |
| Primary constituency | Practitioners and learners, plus teams and customers who benefit from their improved capability |
| Operating horizon | Immediate peer help through durable standards, mentoring networks, and institutional memory |
| Core tension | Encourage open experimentation while curating trustworthy guidance and respecting formal ownership |
| Unique contribution | Connects distributed experience, identifies patterns, and turns them into reusable social and technical infrastructure |
| Success looks like | Members teach and help one another; assets are used and improved; failures are shared safely; expertise is less concentrated |
| Typical failure signal | Attendance and content volume rise while the same questions recur, assets go stale, and real work does not change |

## Core mandate

The Community Leader gives the network a clear purpose, audience, participation model, and connection to real work. They convene useful interactions—demonstrations, office hours, critiques, paired sessions, pattern reviews—and steward the knowledge these produce. They ensure that examples include context, evaluation, limits, and ownership so that reuse does not become cargo cult behavior.

The leader also connects informal discovery to formal systems. A repeated question may require better documentation; a proven pattern may become a standard component; a policy conflict may need a Decision Owner; an unsafe practice may need immediate escalation. The community is a sensing and learning network, not an unaccountable source of enterprise policy.

## Outcomes and motivations

- Practical capability spreads across teams, roles, locations, and levels of prior experience.
- Practitioners can find trustworthy people, examples, evaluations, and guidance when needed.
- Local experiments become reproducible evidence rather than anecdotes.
- Repeated failures and questions improve documentation, platforms, controls, and curricula.
- New mentors and contributors emerge, reducing dependence on founding experts.
- The organization learns from unsuccessful experiments and near misses without shaming participants.
- Formal owners receive a clearer signal about emerging needs, gaps, and effective practices.
- Members develop professional identity, belonging, and confidence during role change.

The Leader is motivated by network capability: making every useful connection and contribution increase what the community can do next.

## Jobs to be done

- **When practitioners are solving similar problems in isolation,** connect them around real artifacts and evidence **so they can learn once and reuse responsibly.**
- **When someone discovers a useful method,** help document context, steps, evaluation, limitations, and ownership **so others can adapt rather than blindly copy it.**
- **When skill levels vary,** provide multiple participation paths and peer support **so novices can enter and experts continue to gain value.**
- **When guidance conflicts or becomes stale,** route it to the correct source owner and make status visible **so the community does not amplify ambiguity.**
- **When an incident or failed experiment teaches something important,** create a psychologically safe learning mechanism **so the lesson improves practice beyond the originating team.**
- **When a pattern matures,** connect it to formal product, process, architecture, risk, or documentation ownership **so adoption has durable support.**
- **When the community depends on a few people,** cultivate facilitators, mentors, curators, and successors **so the network survives turnover and growth.**

## Responsibilities and boundaries

### Owns

- Community purpose, scope, membership experience, working agreements, and participation pathways.
- A useful cadence of practice-centered interactions rather than status broadcasts.
- Stewardship rules for contributed examples, prompts, evaluations, recordings, and discussions.
- Curation signals: authoritative, validated, experimental, deprecated, or disputed.
- Contributor recognition, inclusive facilitation, and leadership succession.
- Measurement and improvement of community value.

### Co-owns

- Learning assets and mentoring networks with teachers, practitioners, and enablement teams.
- Documentation improvements with source owners and Technical Writers.
- Pattern validation with Product, Engineering, Design, Domain, Quality, and Risk experts.
- Enterprise capability insights with transformation and people leaders.

### Contributes to

- Onboarding, evaluation libraries, reusable agent instructions, reference implementations, office hours, and learning events.
- Detection of duplicated tools, unmet platform needs, and inconsistent policy interpretation.
- Cross-team retrospectives and dissemination of incident learning.

### Does not own

- Formal policy, risk acceptance, product roadmaps, performance evaluation, or members' delivery commitments.
- The correctness of every member contribution without an explicit validation process.
- A mandate to force participation or reveal confidential team work.
- Permanent control of the community's voice, agenda, or leadership.
- A central queue for completing work that belongs within product or operational teams.

## Decision rights and escalation triggers

The Community Leader decides facilitation formats, publishing workflow, taxonomy, contribution criteria, moderation, curation state, and use of community resources within the charter. They may remove unsafe, misleading, confidential, abusive, or clearly obsolete material from active recommendation while preserving an auditable reason.

They escalate when a contribution indicates a security, privacy, safety, legal, accessibility, or conduct concern; members treat experimental guidance as policy; authoritative sources conflict; an emerging practice needs formal funding or ownership; confidential information has been shared; participation creates retaliation or performance risk; or the community cannot resolve a material technical or domain dispute. Formal Decision Owners decide standards and risk; the community supplies evidence and implementation experience.

## Inputs and evidence

- Member questions, recurring problems, demonstrations, experiments, reviews, incident lessons, and requests for help.
- Current authoritative documentation, policies, architectural patterns, product roadmaps or bets where relevant, and escalation directories.
- Asset usage, search failures, attendance patterns, contribution diversity, mentoring connections, and qualitative member outcomes.
- Evaluation results, customer and operational evidence, and context about where a practice succeeded or failed.
- Capability gaps identified by teams, leaders, support functions, and communities outside the organization.

Community observation is used to improve the network, not covertly rank employee performance.

## Outputs and artifacts

- A charter defining purpose, audience, scope, norms, roles, and links to formal owners.
- A curated catalog of patterns, prompts, agent instructions, evaluation sets, examples, recordings, and reference implementations.
- Clear asset metadata: owner, source, context, prerequisites, permission needs, evidence, limits, review date, and maturity status.
- Demonstrations, paired working sessions, office hours, critiques, learning challenges, and mentor matching.
- Digestible pattern reports: what practitioners are trying, what works, what fails, and what needs an enterprise decision.
- Contribution and moderation guidance, succession plans, and an archive/deprecation process.
- Proposals to improve documentation, tooling, controls, and formal curricula.

## Operating rhythm

**Continuously:** welcome questions, connect people, curate contributions, correct unsafe guidance, and keep ownership visible.

**Weekly or biweekly:** host an artifact-centered session—demonstration, clinic, paired build, failure review, or critique. Routine announcements remain asynchronous.

**Monthly:** inspect recurring questions, asset usage, unanswered needs, contribution diversity, and emerging practices. Meet only if interaction creates a useful result.

**Quarterly:** validate or deprecate high-use assets, review the charter and measures, rotate facilitators, and deliver a pattern report to formal owners.

**At major tool, policy, or incident changes:** issue a sourced update, identify affected assets, and convene focused learning only where interpretation or practice requires interaction.

## Capabilities and literacies

- Community design, facilitation, moderation, conflict navigation, and volunteer motivation.
- Knowledge management, information architecture, curation, versioning, search, and lifecycle stewardship.
- Adult learning, peer mentoring, demonstration, practice design, and accessible event formats.
- Enough AI and domain literacy to route questions, identify dangerous claims, and recruit qualified reviewers.
- Measurement of network health, contribution patterns, reuse, and practice outcomes without surveillance.
- Organizational navigation: knowing when informal learning must become formal documentation, product work, policy, or risk action.
- Inclusive leadership across time zones, roles, seniority, language, neurotype, disability, and confidence.
- Succession building and willingness to distribute status and authority.

## Mindsets and observable behaviors

- Treats members as contributors, not an audience to be filled with content.
- Leads with real work, evidence, and practice rather than hype or tool announcements.
- Makes uncertainty and maturity explicit: “experimental” is useful information, not a defect.
- Invites newcomers' questions and experts' dissent with equal respect.
- Gives visible credit and preserves source context.
- Connects people directly instead of becoming the permanent intermediary.
- Retires stale material and old rituals even when the leader created them.
- Measures whether work changes, not how many people join a channel.
- Models best intent while enforcing boundaries for harm, confidentiality, and respectful conduct.

## Working with AI

### Appropriate to delegate or augment

- Tagging and search assistance, transcript and discussion summarization, duplicate-question clustering, first-draft asset metadata, accessibility transformations, recommendation of related material, and reminders for review or deprecation.
- A disclosed community assistant may answer routine questions from a bounded, cited knowledge base and route uncertainty to humans.
- Trend detection from approved aggregated community data, with human interpretation.

### Must remain human-accountable

- Community norms, moderation, conflict, qualification of experts, validation or endorsement of practices, sensitive member support, formal escalation, and judgments about organizational meaning.
- Decisions to publish contributions or incident learning that could expose people or confidential work.

### Verification expectations

AI-generated answers cite the authoritative source, show uncertainty, distinguish community experiment from policy, and provide a human escalation route. Summaries are checked for loss of dissent and context. Recommendations do not create a popularity loop that hides minority, accessibility, or high-consequence needs. Member content is used only under clear consent, access, and retention rules.

### Prohibited or constrained uses

No undisclosed bots posing as peers, fabricated expert endorsements, autonomous moderation of sensitive disputes, ranking employees by participation, mining private learning conversations for performance, publishing confidential material, or presenting generated examples as real organizational evidence. An agent cannot promote a practice to “validated” on engagement data alone.

## Experience, tool, and information needs

The Community Leader needs an accessible collaboration space, searchable and versioned knowledge repository, event and office-hour support, approved sandbox, clear source hierarchy, contributor consent model, expert directory, moderation and escalation tools, asset analytics, and time from recognized practitioners. Tooling should allow a member to see what is authoritative, who owns it, when it was reviewed, and how to challenge it.

## Success measures

### Leading indicators

- Questions receive useful peer or sourced responses without depending on one person.
- Contributions come from diverse roles, locations, seniority levels, and experience levels.
- High-use assets include context, evidence, limitations, owner, and review date.
- Sessions include practice, critique, and failure learning—not only demonstrations.
- Members form mentoring and collaboration connections outside the leader.

### Lagging indicators

- Reuse of validated practices with measurable improvement in role or customer outcomes.
- Reduced duplicated experimentation and repeated avoidable errors.
- Faster onboarding and broader independent capability.
- Community-generated patterns become maintained documentation, platform features, evaluations, or standards.
- Leadership and facilitation rotate without loss of momentum.

### Countermetrics

Track stale or unsafe asset use, participation inequality, expert burnout, moderation incidents, time cost, unanswered high-impact questions, forced attendance, confidentiality breaches, popularity bias, and whether the community becomes a substitute for formal product or support ownership.

## Pressures and pain points

- Expectations to serve every audience and tool with limited volunteer capacity.
- Pressure to maximize membership and events instead of practice outcomes.
- Experts who are generous but overloaded; practitioners who consume but do not contribute.
- Fear of sharing failures or unfinished work across hierarchy boundaries.
- Rapid tool and policy change that makes trusted assets stale.
- Tension between open peer exchange and confidential, regulated, or customer-specific context.
- Formal owners who want community energy but do not accept responsibility for mature needs.
- Vendor or internal advocacy that can turn the community into a promotional channel.

## Failure modes and anti-patterns

- **Broadcast community:** posts announcements and recordings but creates few peer connections.
- **Prompt dump:** accumulates decontextualized examples with no evaluation, owner, or lifecycle.
- **Founder gravity:** all questions, events, and decisions depend on one charismatic leader.
- **Demo theater:** showcases ideal successes and suppresses failure or maintenance work.
- **Shadow policy:** community advice is mistaken for an authorized standard.
- **Popularity governance:** high engagement substitutes for correctness or inclusion.
- **Extraction:** asks practitioners to contribute while giving no recognition, time, or reciprocal help.
- **Mandatory voluntarism:** managers force participation, destroying trust and honest learning.
- **Knowledge landfill:** nothing is reviewed, deprecated, consolidated, or made searchable.
- **Center of doing:** the community becomes an unpaid delivery team for work formal owners avoid.

## Guardrails

- Every recommended operational asset shows maturity state, owner, source, evidence, limits, and review date.
- Community guidance never silently overrides formal policy or professional authority.
- Participation, recordings, reuse, and attribution follow clear consent and confidentiality rules.
- Learning data is not repurposed for individual performance ranking.
- Material incidents and unsafe practices follow formal escalation rather than informal debate alone.
- Events and assets meet accessibility requirements and provide asynchronous participation routes.
- Sponsorship cannot purchase positive findings or prohibit good-faith dissent.
- Leadership, moderation, and curation powers are distributed and reviewable.

## Critical scenario

A community member shares an agent instruction that reduces a weekly reporting task from two hours to ten minutes. The post becomes popular and teams begin copying it. Another member discovers that the agent sometimes includes confidential customer details and invents status when a source is missing.

The Community Leader immediately marks the asset “unsafe—do not use,” preserves the evidence, and routes the potential exposure through the incident path. They convene a small artifact clinic with the contributor, Documentation Owner, Security, and practitioners. The group identifies the authoritative data source, adds least-privilege access, missing-source refusal, citation, redaction, evaluation cases, and a human review step. Formal owners decide whether the pattern can become a supported workflow. The community publishes a blameless learning note and corrected validated version. Popularity did not become endorsement; failure became shared infrastructure.

## Representative statements

- “Show the context, evaluation, and failure case—not only the prompt.”
- “Is this community guidance, an experiment, or an authoritative standard?”
- “Who else should be able to answer this without routing through me?”
- “What did not work, and what would make it safe for another team to try?”
- “This pattern is mature enough to need a formal owner.”
- “Attendance is not the outcome; changed, reliable practice is.”

## Maturity progression

| Stage | Observable state |
| --- | --- |
| Reactive | A chat channel and occasional expert presentations answer immediate questions |
| AI-assisted | AI organizes content and drafts answers, but curation, ownership, and contribution remain weak |
| Integrated | The community uses clear lifecycle states, practice events, mentoring, evidence, and formal escalation links |
| AI-native | A distributed human-agent learning network continuously validates, teaches, improves, and transitions practices into maintained organizational infrastructure |

## Definition of ready and done

### A shared practice is ready for community recommendation when

- The problem, audience, prerequisites, permissions, steps, and intended outcome are clear.
- Evidence includes realistic success, boundary, failure, and recovery cases.
- Sources, limitations, owner, maturity state, and next review date are visible.
- Relevant domain, quality, security, legal, accessibility, or product review is complete in proportion to consequence.

### Community stewardship is done when

- Members can find, understand, adapt, evaluate, and challenge the practice without relying on its original author.
- Mature operational needs have a formal owner and support path.
- Stale or unsafe versions are deprecated and discoverably linked to the current source.
- New leaders and maintainers can continue the network without the founding steward.

## Interview and discovery questions

1. What recurring problem brings practitioners together, and what evidence shows a community is the right response?
2. Which questions depend on a few experts today?
3. How can a member distinguish policy, validated pattern, experiment, and personal opinion?
4. What prevents people from sharing failures or asking basic questions safely?
5. Which assets are reused in real work, and what outcomes change?
6. How are high-use materials reviewed, corrected, and retired?
7. Where should community discovery transition to formal product, process, or documentation ownership?
8. Who is missing from participation, contribution, recognition, or leadership?

## Connections to the 15 operating rules

This persona operationalizes **Make Teaching and Learning Part of Everyone's Job** at network scale. It also supports **Treat Documentation as Executable Infrastructure**, **Turn Complaints Into Fixes or Complete Proposals**, **Assume Best Intent**, **Build With a Coordinated Team, Not Alone**, **Stay Flexible in Pursuit of Value**, and **Eliminate Monthly Meetings Unless They Earn Their Place**. The community creates the shared learning system that prevents rapid individual experimentation from fragmenting into private methods, duplicated mistakes, and isolated expertise.
