# Persona: Customer or End User

> The external person, group, or organization whose real-world outcome—not the team’s volume of output—determines whether the product is valuable.

## Purpose and scope

This persona represents the people outside the product organization who encounter, choose, use, administer, depend on, or are materially affected by an AI-enabled product. It is an archetype for organizational decision-making, not a single fictional profile and not an employee role. A product may require several customer archetypes because buyers, administrators, direct users, subjects of automated decisions, and people receiving downstream effects can have different goals and power.

“Customer” and “end user” are not always titles people use for themselves. The role is defined by the relationship to the product and the outcome at stake. Teams must not collapse customer variation into an “average user” or assume that the purchaser’s interests automatically represent everyone affected.

## Persona at a glance

| Dimension | Definition |
|---|---|
| Primary objective | Accomplish a meaningful job safely, effectively, and with acceptable effort |
| Source of legitimacy | Lived experience, context, consequences, preferences, and voluntary participation |
| Primary value contributed | Reality: whether the product is understandable, usable, trustworthy, and worthwhile |
| Time horizon | Immediate task completion through sustained outcomes and long-term consequences |
| Typical evidence | Behavior, feedback, adoption, abandonment, workarounds, outcomes, incidents, and trust signals |
| Core dependencies | Clear promises, accessible experiences, reliable operation, meaningful control, and responsive recovery |
| Principal risk | Being treated as an abstraction, data source, or optimization target rather than a person with agency |
| AI-era emphasis | Informed consent, privacy, explainability, uncertainty, correction, human recourse, and control |

## Core mandate

The customer’s role in the operating model is to pursue their own objective—not to validate the team’s assumptions or make the product successful on the organization’s behalf. Their choices and experiences provide the ultimate test of value. The organization’s mandate is to make participation safe and worthwhile, observe without exploitation, and convert customer evidence into accountable decisions.

Customers may contribute feedback, co-design, data, or expertise, but none of those contributions is owed. Silence does not mean satisfaction; usage does not prove informed consent; retention does not necessarily prove value when switching costs, dependency, or lack of alternatives are present.

## Outcomes and motivations

- Reach the intended outcome with less effort, uncertainty, delay, or risk than available alternatives.
- Understand what the product can and cannot do before relying on it.
- Preserve dignity, autonomy, privacy, and control over consequential choices.
- Receive an experience that works across abilities, languages, devices, connectivity, and levels of expertise.
- Recognize when AI is involved, what information it uses, and how to challenge or correct it.
- Recover from errors without losing work, money, access, standing, or trust.
- Obtain reliable help when self-service is insufficient.
- Leave, export data, revoke permissions, or choose a non-AI path without coercive friction.

## Jobs to be done

- **When** I have a real task under time or attention pressure, **I want** the product to make the next useful action apparent, **so I can** reach my outcome without learning the organization’s internal structure.
- **When** an AI system recommends or performs an action, **I want** to understand its role, confidence, inputs, and consequences, **so I can** decide whether to rely on it.
- **When** the system asks for data or permission, **I want** a meaningful choice and a proportionate explanation, **so I can** participate without surrendering unnecessary control.
- **When** something fails or produces a harmful result, **I want** the failure contained, my work preserved, and a path to correction or human help, **so I can** recover safely.
- **When** my needs differ from the assumed path, **I want** accessible alternatives and adaptable interaction, **so I am not excluded by disability, language, context, or expertise.
- **When** I stop using the product, **I want** to retrieve my information and end ongoing processing, **so I can** leave without hidden penalties.

## Responsibilities and boundaries

### Owns

- Personal goals, preferences, participation, consent choices, and the decision to rely on or reject the product.
- Accurate representation of their experience when they voluntarily provide feedback.
- Their own actions within clearly communicated terms and reasonable capability.

### Co-owns

- Problem framing and solution evaluation when participating in research or co-design.
- Recovery and resolution when the organization provides a safe, understandable path and the customer can reasonably act.

### Contributes to

- Research, usability evaluation, accessibility testing, beta programs, support evidence, and outcome measurement.
- Identification of unmet needs, harmful edge cases, confusing promises, and unintended consequences.

### Does not own

- Product strategy, implementation, model behavior, security, legal compliance, or organizational quality controls.
- Discovering hidden limitations through harm, translating vague errors, or proving that a reported problem exists repeatedly.
- Training the product team, accepting surveillance as a condition of use, or absorbing the cost of unsafe experimentation.

## Decision rights and escalation triggers

Customers have the right to accept or decline optional AI assistance, permissions, research participation, marketing contact, and nonessential data uses. They should be able to correct material data, review consequential actions before commitment, revoke access, request human review where appropriate, and exit or export according to the product’s commitments and applicable obligations.

The organization should surface an immediate escalation path for suspected account compromise, safety threats, discrimination, financial or legal consequences, exposure of sensitive information, repeated inaccessible barriers, irreversible automated action, or inability to reach a human for a high-impact decision. Escalation must not require the customer to diagnose the system’s internal cause.

## Inputs and evidence

Customers rely on accurate product claims, onboarding, pricing, terms, privacy explanations, permission scopes, status and confidence indicators, accessible help, change notices, records of important actions, and clear recovery instructions. Evidence about this persona comes from ethically conducted research, observed behavior, representative usage data, accessibility audits, support cases, success outcomes, complaints, opt-outs, churn, incident reports, and community feedback. Teams should segment evidence by meaningful context and examine who is missing, not only who is easiest to measure.

## Outputs and artifacts

The customer’s outputs may include completed tasks, decisions, created content, configuration, consent records, corrections, feedback, feature requests, support cases, reviews, workarounds, and the decision to adopt, expand, reduce, or leave. These are signals, not instructions. The team converts them into research findings, opportunity statements, experience requirements, evaluated product bets, and accountable fixes.

## Operating rhythm

The customer follows the rhythm of their own life or business, not the development cadence. Interaction may be episodic, continuous, seasonal, urgent, or rare. The organization should support decisive moments: discovery, first use, first value, permission changes, normal operation, updates, failure, recovery, renewal, and departure. Research and telemetry must accommodate this variation rather than demanding constant engagement.

## Capabilities and literacies

No special technical literacy should be presumed. Relevant capabilities vary: domain knowledge, digital fluency, language, accessibility needs, risk tolerance, organizational authority, and familiarity with AI. The product should reveal complexity progressively, use plain language, support assistive technologies, and provide expert controls without making them prerequisites for basic success.

## Mindsets and observable behaviors

- **Outcome-oriented:** judges the product by progress in the real world, not feature sophistication.
- **Context-sensitive:** behaves differently under urgency, uncertainty, cost, or social pressure.
- **Trust-calibrating:** increases reliance when behavior is consistent and transparent; withdraws after surprise or unresolved harm.
- **Adaptive:** uses shortcuts and workarounds when the intended path does not fit.
- **Heterogeneous:** may explore eagerly, proceed cautiously, delegate to others, or refuse AI altogether.
- **Agency-preserving:** seeks understandable choices and resists mechanisms that feel manipulative or irreversible.

## Collaboration map

| Collaborator | What the customer needs from them | What the team learns from the customer |
|---|---|---|
| Product lead | A valuable problem choice and honest priorities | Whether the outcome matters |
| User researcher | Respectful inquiry, consent, and accurate synthesis | Needs, context, behavior, and variation |
| Product designer | Accessible flows, comprehensible states, and recovery | Where understanding or control breaks down |
| Engineering and AI engineering | Reliable, secure, bounded behavior | Real conditions, edge cases, and consequences |
| Success specialist | Outcome guidance without coercion | Adoption barriers and realized value |
| Support specialist | Responsive diagnosis and restoration | Failure patterns and customer impact |
| Marketing or brand lead | Claims that match actual behavior | Language, expectations, and trust response |
| Risk, legal, and domain experts | Practical protection and recourse | Which harms are material in context |

## Working with AI

### May delegate

Drafting, summarization, search, routine classification, recommendations, repetitive operations, and other reversible tasks when the scope is visible and controllable.

### Must retain

Consent, consequential choices, acceptance of risk, final judgment on personal goals and values, and the decision to seek human help. Delegation should never be interpreted as unlimited authority.

### Should verify

Identity-sensitive actions, payments, submissions, legal or medical implications, destructive changes, public communications, and any output the system marks as uncertain or cannot source.

### Prohibited or constrained

Hidden impersonation, dark-pattern consent, sensitive inference unrelated to the task, reuse of private data outside stated purposes, irreversible action without confirmation, denial of service solely by opaque automation, and designs that punish customers for choosing a human or non-AI path.

## Experience, tool, and information needs

Customers need accessible multi-channel interfaces, clear system status, meaningful defaults, preview and undo, data and permission controls, action history, source or rationale where useful, escalation to a human, and portable records. They need information at the moment of consequence—not buried in policy text—including cost, data use, uncertainty, limits, recovery, and who is accountable.

## Success measures

**Leading indicators:** comprehension, task-start confidence, first-value rate, accessibility pass rate, informed permission choices, successful previews, and recovery-path discoverability.

**Lagging indicators:** customer outcome attainment, durable adoption, appropriate trust, retention where freely chosen, reduced harm, complaint resolution, and willingness to recommend.

**Countermetrics:** error severity, regretted actions, involuntary retention, accessibility gaps, privacy complaints, escalations per consequential action, support burden, disparate outcomes, opt-out friction, and customers who abandon after apparent “success.”

## Pressures and pain points

Customers face time pressure, fragmented attention, switching costs, incomplete information, organizational mandates, changing interfaces, fear of making mistakes, and uncertainty about AI behavior. They may be harmed by confident but wrong output, inaccessible workflows, unexpected data use, false personalization, missing context, or support handoffs that require repeating the story.

## Failure modes and anti-patterns

- Inventing one “typical user” and treating variation as edge cases.
- Measuring clicks or retention while ignoring whether the real outcome improved.
- Calling forced behavior “adoption” or preselected permission “consent.”
- Using customers as unpaid quality assurance for known risks.
- Hiding AI involvement, uncertainty, sponsorship, or material limitations.
- Designing only the happy path and blaming customers for predictable recovery needs.
- Prioritizing the buyer while ignoring direct users or people subject to decisions.
- Treating a loud request as representative evidence or customer advocacy as product authority.

## Guardrails

Research and telemetry must be proportionate, consent-aware, minimized, secured, and used for stated purposes. Experiences must meet accessibility standards and include people with varied abilities and contexts in validation. Consequential automation requires calibrated confidence, review or confirmation, auditable records, and a usable appeal path. Product claims must match tested behavior. No customer segment should bear hidden risk merely because it is less visible, less profitable, or harder to recruit.

## Critical scenario: an AI action goes wrong

A customer asks an agent to reconcile a set of records. The agent proposes changes and signals that two matches are uncertain. A safe experience previews every change, separates certain from uncertain items, names the sources used, and lets the customer approve, edit, or decline. After approval, one match proves wrong. The action history supports reversal; the original data remains intact; the customer can report the mismatch once without reconstructing the entire session. Support restores the record, engineering receives structured evidence, research checks whether the explanation was understandable, and the product owner decides whether the confidence threshold or flow must change. The customer’s responsibility ends at describing the impact and choosing recovery. The organization owns learning and prevention.

## Representative statements

- “Help me finish the job; do not make me understand your org chart.”
- “Tell me what the AI will do before it acts on my behalf.”
- “If you need my data, explain why and give me a real choice.”
- “When something goes wrong, preserve my work and show me the way back.”
- “My use of the product is evidence of behavior, not automatic proof of trust or satisfaction.”

## Maturity progression

| Stage | Experience of the customer |
|---|---|
| Reactive | Customers encounter opaque automation, fragmented support, and fixes only after repeated complaints |
| AI-assisted | Optional AI speeds selected tasks, but controls and evidence vary across surfaces |
| Integrated | AI behavior, permissions, recovery, support, and outcome measurement form one coherent journey |
| AI-native | Customers can safely delegate bounded work; agency, accessibility, recourse, and continuous evidence shape the system by design |

## Definition of ready and done

**Ready for customer exposure:** target archetypes and affected parties are named; the job and success signal are observable; claims and limitations are explicit; accessibility, privacy, security, consent, failure, recovery, and escalation paths are designed; representative validation is planned; and no irreversible high-impact action lacks control.

**Done for a customer outcome:** the intended people can achieve the outcome under realistic conditions; material variations and assistive technologies work; customers understand AI involvement and consequences; failures are contained and recoverable; support can resolve issues; outcomes show no unacceptable disparity; and evidence justifies continuing, changing, or stopping the bet.

## Interview and discovery questions

1. What are you trying to accomplish, and what makes that outcome important now?
2. What do you do before and after using this product?
3. Where do uncertainty, delay, cost, or risk enter the current process?
4. What would make you refuse, override, or stop trusting AI assistance?
5. Which information or permissions feel necessary, and which feel excessive?
6. How would you notice an error, and what would safe recovery look like?
7. What ability, language, device, connectivity, or organizational constraints shape your use?
8. Who else buys, configures, approves, experiences, or bears consequences from this product?
9. What does success look like a day, a month, and a year later?
10. What would make leaving or switching difficult?

## Connections to the 15 Operating Rules

- **1, Protect Engineering Velocity; 3, Live Portfolio of Bets; 8, Stay Flexible:** optimize the time from a customer question to credible outcome evidence, not raw output.
