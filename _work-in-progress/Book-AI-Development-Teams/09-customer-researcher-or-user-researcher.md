# Persona: Customer Researcher or User Researcher

> The steward of disciplined inquiry who turns customer behavior and context into decision-ready evidence without pretending that evidence makes the decision.

## Purpose and scope

The Customer Researcher or User Researcher helps an AI-enabled organization understand people as they actually behave: in context, under constraints, across abilities and segments, and over time. This is a reusable role persona rather than a fictional person or generic job description. “Researcher” may be a formal title, a dedicated team function, or a responsibility performed by a trained practitioner embedded in product work. The standards of evidence, consent, privacy, inclusion, and traceability remain necessary regardless of title.

Research is distinct from customer advocacy. The researcher gathers and interprets evidence, identifies uncertainty, and makes implications legible. Product and decision owners choose among competing customer, technical, strategic, ethical, and commercial considerations. Researchers may recommend action, but must separate observation, interpretation, recommendation, and decision.

## Persona at a glance

| Dimension | Definition |
|---|---|
| Primary objective | Reduce consequential uncertainty about customers and affected people |
| Source of authority | Ethical, transparent methods and evidence traceable to participants and context |
| Primary value contributed | Reality-based understanding, including variation, contradiction, and unmet needs |
| Time horizon | Immediate product bets through longitudinal behavior and outcomes |
| Core partners | Customers, product, design, data, support, success, engineering, domain, and risk |
| Principal risk | Becoming a validation service, a proxy for customers, or an advocacy voice detached from evidence |
| AI-era emphasis | Synthetic-data limits, AI-mediated studies, privacy, provenance, and behavioral evaluation |

## Core mandate

Identify what the organization needs to learn, select proportionate methods, recruit the relevant people, conduct ethical inquiry, synthesize findings with calibrated confidence, and connect evidence to a decision while it is still inexpensive to change the product. Preserve disagreement and segment variation rather than averaging away meaningful differences. Maintain a research memory that people and authorized agents can use without exposing participants or stripping findings of context.

## Outcomes and motivations

- Product bets begin with observable customer problems rather than internal preference.
- Teams distinguish what is known, inferred, assumed, and unknown.
- The people most affected—including excluded or low-power groups—are represented in evidence.
- Decisions cite evidence and record where strategy chose to diverge from it.
- Research arrives at the point of decision, not weeks after implementation.
- Repeated studies compound into durable organizational knowledge.
- Participants retain dignity, consent, privacy, and the ability to withdraw.

## Jobs to be done

- **When** a team proposes a customer problem, **I want** to expose the assumptions and decision at stake, **so I can** design the smallest credible inquiry.
- **When** observed behavior conflicts with stated preference or analytics, **I want** to triangulate methods and context, **so I can** explain the contradiction without selecting convenient evidence.
- **When** an AI experience appears useful in a demo, **I want** to observe real users handling uncertainty, permissions, error, and recovery, **so I can** assess whether usefulness survives real conditions.
- **When** participants vary in ability, power, language, or domain expertise, **I want** sampling and methods that reveal those differences, **so I can** prevent an “average user” from concealing exclusion or harm.
- **When** evidence is sufficient for a decision, **I want** to state confidence and implications clearly, **so I can** accelerate action rather than produce a research archive.

## Responsibilities and boundaries

### Owns

- Research questions, method quality, participant protections, consent, sampling rationale, evidence integrity, synthesis traceability, and study limitations.
- Clear separation of observed evidence, interpretation, recommendation, and unresolved uncertainty.
- The research repository’s taxonomy, access controls, retention practices, and contextual metadata when assigned as its steward.

### Co-owns

- Opportunity discovery with product and design.
- Experiment design and success signals with product, data, engineering, and evaluation partners.
- Accessibility and affected-party research with design, legal/risk, and relevant communities.

### Contributes to

- Portfolio bets, experience requirements, model and agent evaluations, journey maps, service recovery, positioning language, and product retrospectives.

### Does not own

- Product strategy, roadmap commitments, architecture, final design choices, marketing claims, compliance rulings, or the decision to ship.
- “The voice of the customer” as a substitute for direct customer access.
- Producing evidence designed to justify a decision already made.

## Decision rights and escalation triggers

The researcher decides whether a proposed method is ethically and methodologically adequate, whether evidence supports a stated conclusion, and whether participant data can be used for a secondary purpose. They may pause or stop a study when consent is compromised, risk exceeds approval, recruiting is coercive, sensitive data is exposed, AI misrepresents itself, or participants experience unanticipated harm.

Escalate when the requested sample systematically excludes affected people; a sponsor asks to suppress inconvenient findings; conclusions exceed the evidence; research data is proposed as model-training material without compatible consent; a high-impact product lacks direct evaluation; or a decision owner repeatedly proceeds without acknowledging material uncertainty.

## Inputs and evidence

Inputs include the decision to be made, current hypotheses, product behavior, prior studies, analytics, support and success themes, market and domain context, incident reports, accessibility findings, and known constraints. Evidence may include interviews, contextual inquiry, diary studies, usability sessions, surveys, concept tests, co-design, longitudinal follow-up, behavioral logs, controlled experiments, and public or secondary research. Strong synthesis triangulates sources, marks provenance, captures disconfirming cases, and distinguishes frequency from severity.

AI-generated synthetic personas, summaries, and simulated participants are not customer evidence. They may help formulate questions or test the research plan, but cannot replace direct contact when human behavior, trust, accessibility, culture, or consequence is material.

## Outputs and artifacts

- Research briefs naming the decision, assumptions, method, sample, risks, and expected use.
- Consent materials, discussion guides, screeners, protocols, and data-management plans.
- Evidence notes, coded observations, clips or quotations with controlled access, and traceable synthesis.
- Findings that state confidence, segment variation, limitations, implications, and open questions.
- Opportunity statements, journey or service maps, usability issues, evaluation cases, and research-backed recommendations.
- Repository entries, decision-record links, readouts, and reusable research operations guidance.

## Operating rhythm

Research works continuously alongside the portfolio of bets. Daily or several times weekly, the researcher joins working-artifact reviews, observes sessions, synthesizes emerging evidence, and flags urgent risks. Weekly, they align upcoming questions with decision dates and share concise findings. At bet checkpoints, they assess whether evidence supports continue, change, expand, or stop. Monthly or quarterly, they audit evidence gaps, recruitment coverage, repository health, research debt, and whether the organization repeatedly studies the same question without learning.

## Capabilities and literacies

Core capabilities include qualitative and quantitative methods, sampling, interviewing, observation, facilitation, accessibility, ethics, privacy, bias awareness, synthesis, storytelling with evidence, experiment literacy, basic analytics, and organizational influence. AI literacy includes understanding probabilistic behavior, automation bias, hallucination, model and prompt changes, evaluation design, provenance, and the risks of allowing AI tools to process participant data.

## Mindsets and observable behaviors

- **Curious, not prosecutorial:** explores why behavior occurs instead of forcing a preferred interpretation.
- **Evidence-calibrated:** says “we observed,” “we infer,” and “we do not know” precisely.
- **Decision-oriented:** starts from the uncertainty that blocks action and works backward to the method.
- **Inclusive by design:** looks for missing people, power differences, accessibility needs, and downstream subjects.
- **Comfortable with contradiction:** preserves minority and disconfirming evidence when it changes risk.
- **Humble about representation:** creates access to customers rather than claiming to speak for all of them.

## Working with AI

### May delegate

Transcription in approved environments, de-identification assistance, scheduling, initial coding suggestions, repository search, draft guides, theme clustering, and formatting. Automation should reduce clerical effort, not obscure method.

### Must retain

Participant relationship, consent judgment, sensitive interviewing, sampling decisions, interpretation, ethical tradeoffs, confidence calibration, and the distinction between evidence and advocacy.

### Must verify

Every AI-generated transcript, quotation, code, theme, translation, redaction, and summary against source material. Researchers should test for erased disagreement, fabricated details, misattributed speakers, and cultural or accessibility distortion.

### Prohibited or constrained

Uploading participant data to unapproved models; training models on research material without explicit compatible authorization; generating synthetic quotations and presenting them as evidence; automated emotion or demographic inference without a justified, reviewed basis; undisclosed bots posing as researchers; and allowing AI to contact or act on participants without oversight.

## Experience, tool, and information needs

Researchers need direct product access, prototypes, change logs, analytics, a secure participant and consent system, approved recording/transcription, accessible research tools, protected repositories, tagging and provenance, decision records, and visibility into support, success, and incident data. They need lead time proportional to the question, but also lightweight methods that fit rapid product cycles. Decision owners must state when and how findings will be used.

## Success measures

**Leading indicators:** percentage of bets with a named evidence gap and research plan; time from question to decision-ready evidence; sample coverage; participant consent quality; decision-owner attendance; and reuse of prior findings.

**Lagging indicators:** fewer invalid assumptions reaching production; better customer outcomes; improved accessibility and task success; evidence-cited decisions; reduced repeated research; and earlier detection of harmful or unwanted behavior.

**Countermetrics:** study volume without decisions, participant burden, recruitment concentration, privacy incidents, findings later contradicted because limitations were hidden, suppressed negative evidence, and research that only validates shipped designs.

## Pressures and pain points

The role is pressured to move faster than recruiting allows, compress complex findings into a slogan, prove demand, speak for every customer, or accept convenient participants as representative. Fragmented repositories, weak analytics, changing prototypes, unclear decisions, restricted customer access, and stakeholders who value anecdotes only when supportive all reduce impact.

## Failure modes and anti-patterns

- Treating advocacy, preference, or stakeholder opinion as equivalent to evidence.
- Asking leading questions designed to confirm a concept.
- Reporting frequency without severity, or quotations without sample context.
- Averaging away accessibility needs, minority behavior, or downstream harm.
- Producing polished readouts after the decision window has closed.
- Becoming the sole gateway to customers and insulating builders from direct observation.
- Allowing AI summaries to replace raw-evidence review.
- Treating absence of complaints as proof of satisfaction.

## Guardrails

Every study must name purpose, use, consent basis, retention, access, and withdrawal path. Collect the minimum sensitive data required. Protect identities and avoid combining datasets in ways participants would not reasonably expect. Compensate fairly and avoid coercive recruitment. Report limitations and disconfirming evidence. High-impact or vulnerable-population research requires additional review, specialist involvement, and a plan for responding to disclosures or harm.

## Critical scenario: evidence challenges an attractive bet

A team has a polished agent prototype and strong internal enthusiasm. Analytics suggest users abandon during permission setup. The researcher observes participants and finds three different causes: some misunderstand the requested scope, assistive-technology users cannot navigate the confirmation flow, and administrators fear that the agent can act beyond the current task. The researcher reports observed behavior, the inferred trust model, sample limits, and three implications. They do not declare the product canceled. The product owner pauses expansion, design repairs the permission and accessibility model, engineering narrows authority, and the team defines a new customer test. Evidence changed the bet without being inflated into unilateral authority.

## Representative statements

- “What decision will this research change?”
- “That is an interpretation; here is the behavior we actually observed.”
- “Five participants found the same barrier, but this study does not estimate population frequency.”
- “Synthetic users can challenge our guide; they cannot tell us what customers experienced.”
- “The minority case matters because the consequence is severe.”

## Maturity progression

| Stage | Practice |
|---|---|
| Reactive | Research occurs after complaints or near launch and mainly validates solutions |
| AI-assisted | Approved AI reduces research administration, but evidence remains fragmented |
| Integrated | Research questions, customer access, product bets, evaluations, and decisions share a continuous loop |
| AI-native | Durable, permissioned evidence informs people and agents at decision time while human researchers protect meaning, context, and participants |

## Definition of ready and done

**Ready to research:** the decision and uncertainty are explicit; prior evidence has been reviewed; affected groups and sampling limits are named; method and analysis fit the question; consent, privacy, accessibility, compensation, retention, and escalation are approved; and the decision owner commits to receiving results.

**Done:** source material is secured and traceable; observations, interpretations, and recommendations are separated; variation, limitations, confidence, and disconfirming cases are reported; urgent findings are escalated; the decision and rationale are recorded; participants are closed out appropriately; and reusable evidence is deposited with permissions and context intact.

## Interview and discovery questions

1. What decision are we trying to make, and by when?
2. Which assumptions would most change that decision if false?
3. Whose behavior or outcome matters, and who may be missing from our sample?
4. What direct, behavioral, quantitative, or secondary evidence already exists?
5. What are the consequences of a false positive and a false negative?
6. How might AI involvement change trust, disclosure, performance, or consent?
7. Which conditions must we observe beyond the happy path?
8. What evidence would cause us to stop, narrow, or redesign the bet?
9. Who will own the response to findings?
10. How will future teams know the limits and provenance of this evidence?
