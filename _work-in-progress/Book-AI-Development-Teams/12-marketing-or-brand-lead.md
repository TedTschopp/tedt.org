# Persona: Marketing or Brand Lead

> The steward of market meaning and promise integrity who ensures that what the organization says is compelling, recognizable, and true in the product customers actually experience.

## Purpose and scope

The Marketing or Brand Lead defines how an AI-enabled product is understood, for whom it matters, why it is distinct, and what the organization can credibly promise. This is a reusable organizational role persona rather than a fictional person or generic job description. Titles may divide the work among product marketing, brand strategy, communications, content, demand generation, or creative leadership. The persona captures their shared accountability for coherent positioning and trustworthy expression.

Brand is not merely visual consistency, and marketing is not permission to outrun the product. Claims must match observable behavior across the interface, agent, API, sales motion, onboarding, support, pricing, privacy practice, and recovery experience. A customer’s encounter with the product is part of the brand whether marketing created that surface or not.

## Persona at a glance

| Dimension | Definition |
|---|---|
| Primary objective | Create clear, differentiated demand without breaking the product promise |
| Source of authority | Market insight, brand system, claim evidence, audience understanding, and channel expertise |
| Primary value contributed | Connects customer language, product truth, and organizational identity |
| Time horizon | Immediate campaigns through long-term reputation and category position |
| Core partners | Product, research, design, sales, customer teams, legal/risk, and executives |
| Principal risk | Optimizing attention or conversion with claims the product, evidence, or operations cannot sustain |
| AI-era emphasis | Disclosure, provenance, synthetic-media controls, claim evaluation, and scalable personalization |

## Core mandate

Identify and prioritize audiences; develop evidence-based positioning and messaging; maintain a usable brand system; plan responsible launches and lifecycle communications; ensure claims are substantiated and reflected in product behavior; equip customer-facing teams with accurate language; measure comprehension and trust as well as reach; and convert market response into learning for the portfolio of bets.

## Outcomes and motivations

- The intended audience recognizes the problem, understands the offered outcome, and can decide whether the product fits.
- Positioning is differentiated, specific, accessible, and resilient under scrutiny.
- Every material claim has an owner, source, scope, limitation, and review date.
- Product behavior, pricing, sales commitments, support, and brand promise remain coherent.
- AI-generated communications preserve voice, facts, rights, consent, and cultural context.
- Launches create a learning loop rather than a one-time burst of attention.
- Trust compounds because the organization corrects mistakes openly and avoids manipulative tactics.

## Jobs to be done

- **When** the team chooses a product bet, **I want** to define the audience, category, problem, alternative, and provable difference, **so I can** test whether the market understands and values it.
- **When** a capability is approaching launch, **I want** to compare every proposed claim with tested behavior and operational readiness, **so I can** avoid promising an experience the organization cannot deliver.
- **When** AI generates content or personalization at scale, **I want** enforceable brand, evidence, consent, and disclosure rules, **so I can** gain speed without multiplying reputational risk.
- **When** market response differs from the hypothesis, **I want** to separate reach, comprehension, intent, product fit, and delivery failure, **so I can** recommend the right change.
- **When** a public error occurs, **I want** a verified correction with accountable follow-through, **so I can** restore informed trust rather than defend a false impression.

## Responsibilities and boundaries

### Owns

- Positioning, messaging architecture, audience definitions, brand voice and identity governance, channel strategy, campaign and content quality, claim inventory, and brand measurement.
- Clear labeling and review standards for AI-generated or synthetic media in marketing contexts.
- Consistency and accessibility of approved public expression.

### Co-owns

- Launch readiness and lifecycle communication with product, customer teams, operations, and legal/risk.
- Customer language and market insight with research and sales.
- Product promise and proof with product and evidence owners.
- Crisis communication with executive, incident, legal, security, and customer leaders.

### Contributes to

- Portfolio-bet framing, naming, packaging, pricing research, onboarding language, product voice, education, recruitment messaging, and community engagement.

### Does not own

- Product behavior, architecture, final legal interpretation, sales commitments, customer outcome delivery, or whether a product should ship.
- Declaring a capability safe, compliant, unbiased, autonomous, or accurate without authoritative evidence.
- Editing away limitations that materially affect a customer’s choice.

## Decision rights and escalation triggers

The lead decides approved positioning, brand expression, channel fit, editorial standards, and whether evidence is adequate for a marketing claim. They may pause or withdraw a campaign, asset, endorsement, or launch message when the claim no longer matches behavior, required rights are missing, synthetic media is misleading, or the customer experience cannot fulfill the promise.

Escalate when teams request absolute or comparative claims without proof; AI output uses protected material or personal likeness without rights; targeting relies on sensitive inference; product behavior changes after approval; sales language exceeds public commitments; dark patterns undermine informed choice; an incident contradicts core promises; or speed pressure would bypass legal, accessibility, security, or evidence review.

## Inputs and evidence

Inputs include product strategy and working behavior, customer research, market and competitor evidence, support and success themes, approved pricing and packaging, evaluation results, reliability and security evidence, brand strategy, legal requirements, rights and consent records, channel performance, and current commitments. Claims should rely on authoritative, current evidence. Qualitative resonance, conversion, and social response are useful but do not prove product outcomes or technical properties.

## Outputs and artifacts

- Positioning statement, audience and problem definition, category frame, value proposition, and message hierarchy.
- Brand principles, voice guidance, visual system, terminology, and accessible content standards.
- Claim register containing wording, evidence, scope, limitations, owner, approval, and expiry.
- Launch brief, channel plan, content, sales and support enablement, FAQs, and change communications.
- AI-content instructions, approved-source hierarchy, disclosure and provenance requirements, and review tiers.
- Market-learning report connecting message, audience, behavior, product experience, and decision.
- Correction or crisis communication with facts, impact, response, and next update.

## Operating rhythm

Daily, the lead reviews active market signals, product changes affecting claims, and high-risk generated content. Weekly, marketing joins the working product artifact, tests messages with research and customer teams, and aligns launch dependencies. At each portfolio decision, it updates audience, promise, proof, and learning plan. Monthly or quarterly, it audits claim validity, brand consistency, accessibility, content provenance, channel quality, and reputation indicators. Recurring status becomes a dashboard; meetings are reserved for creative critique, ambiguity, high-impact decisions, and crisis coordination.

## Capabilities and literacies

Capabilities include positioning, market research, customer language analysis, narrative, copy and editorial judgment, visual and verbal brand systems, channel strategy, experimentation, accessibility, measurement, stakeholder alignment, rights management, and crisis communication. AI literacy includes model limitations, provenance, synthetic media, copyright and likeness risk, privacy-aware personalization, generated-content detection limits, prompt and agent governance, and claim evaluation.

## Mindsets and observable behaviors

- **Promise-centered:** treats every message as an expectation the full organization must fulfill.
- **Evidence-seeking:** prefers a narrower true claim over a broad unprovable one.
- **Audience-empathetic:** uses customer language without exploiting fear, confusion, or vulnerability.
- **Coherent:** aligns visual, verbal, behavioral, and service signals.
- **Distinctive with restraint:** creates memorable expression without manufacturing certainty.
- **Correction-ready:** updates or retracts claims quickly when evidence changes.

## Working with AI

### May delegate

Variations of approved copy, summarization, localization drafts, content tagging, research synthesis assistance, asset resizing, channel adaptation, accessibility checks, performance pattern detection, and retrieval from approved brand and claim sources.

### Must retain

Positioning, taste, cultural and emotional judgment, claim approval, crisis communication, sensitive audience decisions, ethical boundaries, and accountability for what is published.

### Must verify

Facts, quotations, sources, product behavior, comparative claims, prices, rights, consent, translations, accessibility, generated images, endorsements, links, and current approvals. High-reach output requires proportionately stronger review.

### Prohibited or constrained

Fabricated customers, testimonials, reviews, citations, or endorsements; undeclared material synthetic representation; cloning voices or likenesses without rights; sensitive-trait inference for targeting; deceptive scarcity or anthropomorphic claims; autonomous publishing in high-risk channels; and content derived from confidential data without authorization.

## Experience, tool, and information needs

The role needs direct access to the working product, current evaluations, research repositories, customer evidence, launch dependencies, incident and change alerts, approved asset and rights libraries, brand tokens, claim registry, experiment results, and publication audit trails. Content systems should enforce source hierarchy, expiry, review tier, accessibility, provenance, and rollback. Teams need a fast path for bounded low-risk content and deliberate review for consequential or high-reach claims.

## Success measures

**Leading indicators:** message comprehension, qualified audience fit, claim coverage and freshness, launch-dependency readiness, accessible-content compliance, source-linked AI output, and time to update after product change.

**Lagging indicators:** trust, preference, qualified demand, conversion followed by successful product outcomes, retention consistent with the promise, reputation resilience, and earned advocacy.

**Countermetrics:** complaint and refund reasons tied to misleading expectations, claim corrections, sales/product mismatch, unsubscribes and opt-outs, dark-pattern dependence, rights or privacy incidents, inaccessible content, low-quality generated-content volume, and conversion that produces rapid churn or support burden.

## Pressures and pain points

Marketing faces pressure for constant content, immediate launch, category leadership, short-term conversion, and certainty before evidence is mature. Product changes can invalidate approved copy overnight. Fragmented claims across web, sales, agents, app stores, and partners create drift. AI makes plausible variation cheap, increasing review volume, sameness, fabricated detail, rights uncertainty, and the temptation to optimize attention without preserving trust.

## Failure modes and anti-patterns

- Describing a prototype, aspiration, or limited beta as generally available behavior.
- Calling probabilistic assistance “fully autonomous,” “error-free,” or “human-level” without defined evidence.
- Optimizing click-through while downstream customers fail or churn.
- Using customer language as decorative quotation without context or permission.
- Treating brand as logo policing while product errors and support behavior break the promise.
- Publishing large volumes of generic AI content that erode distinctiveness.
- Allowing different channels or sales teams to invent incompatible claims.
- Responding to a public error with defensive ambiguity rather than verified correction.

## Guardrails

Every material claim requires evidence, scope, limitations, owner, approval, and expiry. Marketing must distinguish current capability, preview, plan, and aspiration. Personalization uses consented, minimized data and never exploits sensitive vulnerability. Synthetic media and AI interaction receive disclosure proportionate to the likelihood of deception. Rights, attribution, accessibility, privacy, and channel rules are non-negotiable. Product and operational owners must confirm that the promised experience is deliverable before launch.

## Critical scenario: the promise outruns behavior

A launch campaign describes an agent as completing a regulated workflow “autonomously.” Evaluation shows that it succeeds only with a narrow data format and requires expert review for exceptions. The marketing lead stops the absolute claim, observes the working product, and works with product, domain, risk, and research to define truthful language: the agent prepares and validates routine cases, flags exceptions, cites sources, and leaves final submission to an authorized person. The claim register links to the evaluation and names its scope. Sales and support receive the same language. The narrower promise increases qualified understanding and prevents the brand from turning an implementation constraint into customer harm.

## Representative statements

- “What can a customer reliably experience today?”
- “A memorable promise must also be an operational promise.”
- “That result supports a scoped claim, not an absolute one.”
- “AI can multiply variants; it cannot own our taste, rights, or truth.”
- “If support must explain why the message was misleading, the brand has already failed.”

## Maturity progression

| Stage | Practice |
|---|---|
| Reactive | Brand review is late, claims live in scattered assets, and corrections follow complaints |
| AI-assisted | Teams generate content faster but rely on manual review and fragmented proof |
| Integrated | Product behavior, claim evidence, brand systems, channels, and customer feedback share one governed loop |
| AI-native | Agents create and adapt bounded expression from executable brand and claim infrastructure while humans retain promise, taste, and ethical judgment |

## Definition of ready and done

**Ready to market:** audience, problem, outcome, category, and alternative are explicit; product behavior and operational capacity are verified; claims have evidence, scope, limitations, owner, and approval; pricing, privacy, accessibility, support, rights, disclosure, and change plans are aligned; and success plus countermetrics are defined.

**Done for a launch or campaign:** approved assets are published with provenance; all customer-facing teams use coherent language; monitoring covers comprehension, outcome, risk, and brand response; discrepancies are corrected across channels; evidence and decisions are archived; and the portfolio owner has enough learning to continue, modify, expand, or stop.

## Interview and discovery questions

1. Who is the intended audience, and who should recognize that this is not for them?
2. What problem and moment of value can customers describe in their own language?
3. Which alternative are they using now, and why would they change?
4. What can the product reliably do today under realistic conditions?
5. What evidence supports each material claim, and when does it expire?
6. Which limitation would change an informed customer’s decision?
7. Where could AI-generated content create deception, rights, privacy, or cultural risk?
8. Can sales, onboarding, product, support, and recovery fulfill the same promise?
9. Which metric would reveal attention without value or conversion without fit?
10. What will we say and do if behavior changes or the claim proves wrong?

## Connections to the 15 Operating Rules

- **1, Protect Engineering Velocity:** use current product evidence and reusable claims instead of repeated launch interruption.
- **2 and 7, Meeting Limits:** move approvals, claims, and status into durable workflows; reserve meetings for creative and risk decisions.
- **3, Live Portfolio of Bets; 8, Stay Flexible:** treat positioning and channels as testable hypotheses and revise them with evidence.
- **4 and 6, Working Environment and Daily Jam:** let marketing inspect the real artifact before setting expectations.
- **9, Assume Best Intent; 10, Turn Complaints Into Fixes:** interpret market feedback carefully and respond to mismatches with complete corrective proposals.
- **11, Documentation as Executable Infrastructure:** make brand rules, claims, sources, permissions, and expiry machine-usable.
- **12, Expand Design Beyond Screens; 13, One Exceptional Customer Experience:** align message with AI behavior, errors, permissions, support, and recovery.
- **14, Coordinated Team; 15, Teaching and Learning:** make trustworthy expression a cross-functional, continuously learned capability.
