# Persona: Learner or AI Practitioner

> Builds practical judgment through responsible experimentation, verification, reflection, and contribution—then helps the organization learn from the evidence.

## Purpose and scope

This persona applies to anyone developing capability in AI-enabled work, from a first-time user to an experienced practitioner entering a new domain, model, agent pattern, or responsibility. “Learner” describes a stance, not seniority. “AI Practitioner” describes someone applying AI to real work, not necessarily an engineer or data scientist. A person can be expert in their profession and still be a learner in this operating model.

The role is active. The Learner does not wait passively for perfect training, nor experiment carelessly with organizational data and customer outcomes. They choose useful problems, work inside approved boundaries, test results, seek feedback, document lessons, and progressively take responsibility for more complex work.

## Persona at a glance

| Dimension | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Primary mission | Develop and apply reliable AI-enabled working practices that improve real outcomes |
| Primary constituency | The customers and colleagues affected by the work, plus the community that can learn from it |
| Operating horizon | The next safe experiment through durable professional adaptability |
| Core tension | Move from curiosity to useful practice without mistaking fluent output for competence |
| Unique contribution | Brings a learner's questions and frontline experience together, exposing gaps experts may no longer see |
| Success looks like | Chooses appropriate use cases, supplies context, evaluates output, escalates limits, improves workflows, and teaches evidence-backed lessons |
| Typical failure signal | Copies outputs or prompts without verification, hides AI use, repeats unsafe experiments, or consumes training without changing practice |

## Core mandate

The Learner owns their participation in capability building. They identify a role-relevant job, understand the permitted environment, attempt the work, evaluate what happened, seek critique, and record enough context for another person to reproduce or challenge the lesson.

The mandate is not to use AI everywhere. Mature practice includes recognizing tasks for which automation adds little value, evidence is insufficient, the consequences are too high, or human presence is itself part of the desired experience. The Learner preserves professional accountability while exploring how people and agents can divide work more effectively.

## Outcomes and motivations

- Greater ability to solve meaningful problems, not merely generate more artifacts.
- Confidence grounded in observable practice and verification.
- A personal library of patterns, evaluations, failure lessons, and escalation routes.
- Less time spent on repeatable coordination and more on judgment, customers, craft, and learning.
- Constructive participation in product, process, and documentation improvement.
- Recognition and growth based on contribution, teaching, and responsible experimentation.
- Adaptability as tools, roles, and organizational expectations change.

Motivations vary: curiosity, service to customers, professional relevance, reduced drudgery, creative leverage, advancement, or fear of being left behind. A healthy learning system acknowledges these differences instead of demanding performative enthusiasm.

## Jobs to be done

- **When I encounter a repeatable or difficult part of my work,** frame the job, constraints, and desired outcome **so I can judge whether AI assistance is appropriate.**
- **When I use a model or agent,** provide the right context, sources, permissions, and stop conditions **so it can help without silently exceeding my authority.**
- **When output appears convincing,** compare it with authoritative evidence and realistic tests **so I do not transfer machine confidence into a human decision.**
- **When I reach a limit,** ask a focused question or escalate with evidence **so another person can help without reconstructing the entire situation.**
- **When an experiment succeeds or fails,** record the setup, result, caveats, and next decision **so the lesson can be reproduced and challenged.**
- **When I learn a useful method,** improve shared documentation or teach a peer **so the capability does not remain private.**
- **When the environment changes,** revisit assumptions and evaluation **so yesterday's reliable practice does not become today's hidden defect.**

## Responsibilities and boundaries

### Owns

- Choosing a meaningful, authorized learning objective and understanding relevant policies.
- The quality and consequences of work submitted under their name or role.
- Verification proportionate to consequence, including source checks and independent tests.
- Accurate disclosure of material AI assistance where required or useful for review.
- Protecting customer, employee, proprietary, regulated, and confidential information.
- Seeking help, reporting failures or near misses, and stopping when outside authority.
- Reflection, documentation, and contribution of reusable lessons.

### Co-owns

- Development goals and practice opportunities with a Mentor or People Manager.
- Workflow redesign with Process Owners and affected colleagues.
- Evaluation criteria with domain, quality, product, and risk experts.
- Improvements to documentation, prompts, examples, and community assets.

### Contributes to

- Peer reviews, demonstrations, experiments, retrospectives, customer learning, and communities of practice.
- Honest feedback about tool access, friction, inclusion, and unintended consequences.

### Does not own

- Authority the organization has not delegated, even if an agent can technically perform the action.
- Unilateral deployment of high-consequence automation.
- The right to expose data, bypass controls, or ignore intellectual-property obligations in the name of learning.
- Treating an AI answer as approval from Security, Legal, a domain professional, or a Decision Owner.
- An obligation to adopt every new tool or to conceal legitimate concern about role change.

## Decision rights and escalation triggers

Within approved boundaries, the Learner can select experiments, compare methods, create low-risk prototypes, and decide what to submit for critique. They decide whether a tool is useful for their own authorized task, subject to organizational standards and accountable review.

They stop and escalate when data classification is unclear; tool or agent permissions exceed the task; output may affect safety, rights, money, regulated decisions, production systems, or external communications; sources conflict; verification is unavailable; an agent behaves unexpectedly; a policy appears contradictory; or pressure encourages hidden or misleading use. Escalation includes intent, inputs, observed behavior, evidence, possible impact, actions already taken, and the requested help.

## Inputs and evidence

- A real job to be done, intended beneficiary, constraints, baseline, and definition of a useful result.
- Authoritative documentation, policies, examples, evaluation criteria, and known failure modes.
- Approved tools, safe data, permission scope, sandbox environments, and access to support.
- Feedback from mentors, peers, domain experts, customers, and operational signals.
- The Learner's own record of prompts or instructions, tool calls, source material, changes, results, and uncertainty.

## Outputs and artifacts

- A working artifact or changed behavior connected to a real outcome.
- Experiment notes: hypothesis, setup, data classification, result, evaluation, limitation, and next action.
- Reusable prompts or agent instructions with context, sources, permissions, evaluation, and failure handling.
- Corrections or additions to operational documentation.
- Demonstrations that include what failed and how the output was tested.
- Focused questions, issue reports, complete proposals, and responsible escalations.
- A personal development plan and evidence of increasing independence.

## Operating rhythm

**Before an experiment:** define the job, consequence, authorization, baseline, source of truth, and evidence of success. Choose the smallest safe test.

**During:** keep important inputs and transformations traceable; inspect intermediate behavior; do not grant broader permissions merely to overcome friction.

**After:** test the artifact, seek critique, compare with the baseline, record limitations, and decide whether to stop, adapt, repeat, or propose wider use.

**Weekly:** practice a role-relevant skill, review a failure or boundary case, and contribute at least one useful question, example, or correction when warranted.

**Periodically:** teach back a practice, reassess old workflows as models and policies change, and choose the next capability gap based on real work.

## Capabilities and literacies

- Problem framing: beneficiary, job, evidence, constraints, and smallest useful test.
- Basic AI concepts: probabilistic output, context limits, tool use, agents, retrieval, model variability, and drift.
- Prompt and instruction design as clear specification, not incantation.
- Source evaluation, fact checking, testing, comparison, and calibration of confidence.
- Data classification, privacy, security, intellectual property, accessibility, and role-specific obligations.
- Workflow mapping and recognition of when to automate, augment, redesign, or eliminate.
- Clear written communication, feedback seeking, reflection, and teach-back.
- Emotional adaptability: tolerating beginner status, uncertainty, critique, and changing professional identity.

## Mindsets and observable behaviors

- Starts with a valuable job, not a desire to use a particular model.
- Remains curious and appropriately skeptical at the same time.
- Treats generated output as material to inspect, not an answer to obey.
- Makes uncertainty and assumptions visible.
- Learns in public within safe boundaries and shares failures without dramatizing them.
- Asks focused questions after attempting to locate the authoritative source.
- Changes method when evidence changes and avoids defending sunk effort.
- Respects colleagues who adopt at different rates or raise legitimate concerns.
- Gives credit to people, sources, and prior work rather than attributing everything to the tool.

## Working with AI

### Appropriate to delegate or augment

- Low-consequence drafting, summarization of approved material, brainstorming, transformation between formats, test generation, critique against a rubric, practice conversations, exploration, and routine workflow steps within explicit permissions.
- Agents may execute defined, observable tasks with least privilege, stop conditions, logging, and human escalation.

### Must remain human-accountable

- Framing the real problem, choosing the source of truth, judging consequence, validating final work, disclosing material limitations, obtaining approval, and accepting professional responsibility.
- Empathy, values, sensitive stakeholder communication, and decisions about rights, safety, employment, or significant access.

### Verification expectations

Verification scales with consequence. Low-risk internal drafts may need a quick source check; code needs tests and review; customer claims need evidence and authorization; high-impact decisions require independent qualified review, auditability, and often non-AI corroboration. The Learner records enough of the process for the reviewer to understand what was generated, transformed, and checked.

### Prohibited or constrained uses

No unapproved sensitive data, credential sharing, evasion of safeguards, fabricated sources or customer evidence, hidden impersonation, autonomous high-impact decisions, or deployment beyond delegated authority. Do not use AI to conceal lack of understanding in work for which the Learner is accountable.

## Experience, tool, and information needs

The Learner needs a clear approved-tool catalog; readable policy; data-classification examples; a safe sandbox; representative tasks; current documentation; evaluation checklists; mentors and office hours; accessible training formats; and visible examples of responsible failures as well as successes. They need protected time and psychological safety. A license without context, practice, or review is access—not enablement.

## Success measures

### Leading indicators

- Experiments begin with a problem, boundary, baseline, and evaluation plan.
- The Learner can explain sources, assumptions, permissions, and tests.
- Feedback is requested earlier, and boundary cases are practiced.
- Lessons become documented improvements or peer teaching.
- AI is deliberately not used where it would reduce trust or add needless complexity.

### Lagging indicators

- Independent completion of increasingly varied, role-relevant work.
- Improved customer, quality, cycle-time, or employee outcomes attributable to changed practice.
- Fewer repeat errors, unsafe uses, and escalations caused by missing basics.
- Contribution to team capability rather than personal output alone.

### Countermetrics

Track rework, overconfidence, policy violations, hidden labor shifted to reviewers, customer or colleague harm, tool dependence, learning inequity, after-hours experimentation, and whether artifact volume rises without outcome improvement.

## Pressures and pain points

- Fear of appearing obsolete, slow, naive, or insufficiently enthusiastic.
- Conflicting advice, rapidly changing tools, and unclear source hierarchy.
- Polished output that makes gaps in understanding difficult to detect.
- Lack of safe data, realistic examples, access, or time to practice.
- Performance systems that reward delivery while treating learning as extra work.
- Social pressure either to use AI for everything or to reject it categorically.
- Difficulty knowing when an experiment becomes an operational or regulated activity.

## Failure modes and anti-patterns

- **Copy-submit:** forwards generated work without understanding or verification.
- **Prompt collection:** accumulates recipes but cannot adapt them to new situations.
- **Tool-first wandering:** searches for uses of a product rather than valuable jobs.
- **Secret use:** hides material AI assistance, preventing appropriate review and learning.
- **Unsafe curiosity:** uses real sensitive data or broad permissions in casual experiments.
- **Automation escalation:** responds to failure by granting more access or autonomy.
- **Confidence theater:** performs certainty to appear advanced and suppresses questions.
- **Solo optimization:** improves personal speed while shifting verification, cleanup, or risk to others.
- **Permanent novice:** repeatedly requests rescue without practicing or documenting the answer.

## Guardrails

- Know the data, tool, permission, and external-disclosure boundaries before using AI.
- Remain accountable for work submitted or actions initiated under your identity.
- Match verification and review to consequence; when unsure, narrow the test or escalate.
- Use a sandbox or synthetic data for learning whenever real-world impact is unnecessary.
- Tell reviewers what they need to know about generated or transformed material.
- Record and report unexpected agent behavior, near misses, and material errors.
- Preserve accessibility, attribution, consent, privacy, and intellectual-property obligations.
- Do not let urgency convert a learning experiment into an unowned production system.

## Critical scenario

A Learner uses an approved assistant to summarize customer-support cases and discovers what appears to be a strong pattern. The assistant recommends an automated account restriction for a category of customers. The summary is persuasive, but the cases contain sensitive data, the category may proxy for a protected group, and the Learner has no authority to change account access.

The Learner stops before turning the recommendation into a workflow. They verify that the data use is permitted, inspect representative cases, identify missing base-rate and bias evidence, and document the assistant's unsupported causal leap. They ask the Support owner, Researcher, Domain Expert, and Risk reviewer to examine the underlying problem. The team reframes the opportunity as improving a confusing recovery path, conducts a bounded test, and creates an evaluation set. The Learner's contribution is valuable precisely because they distinguish discovery from authority and a plausible pattern from a justified decision.

## Representative statements

- “What job am I improving, and how will the beneficiary notice?”
- “This output is plausible; here is how I checked it and where uncertainty remains.”
- “The agent can perform the step, but I do not have authority to authorize the outcome.”
- “I tried two approaches, captured the failure, and need help with this specific boundary.”
- “This is not a safe use of real data; I can test the method with a synthetic case.”
- “What should I add to the shared guidance so the next person starts further ahead?”

## Maturity progression

| Stage | Observable state |
| --- | --- |
| Reactive | Uses or avoids AI based on urgency, novelty, or fear; verification and disclosure are inconsistent |
| AI-assisted | Uses approved tools for individual tasks and follows checklists, but workflows and learning remain personal |
| Integrated | Selects by job and consequence, evaluates systematically, redesigns workflows, and contributes reusable evidence |
| AI-native | Fluidly coordinates people and agents, preserves accountability, adapts as evidence changes, and grows capability by teaching others |

## Definition of ready and done

### An experiment is ready when

- The job, beneficiary, baseline, hypothesis, smallest safe test, and success signal are clear.
- Tool, data, permission, review, and disclosure requirements are understood.
- The source of truth, verification method, stop conditions, and escalation path exist.

### A learning cycle is done when

- The result has been evaluated against the intended outcome and relevant countermetrics.
- Material assumptions, failures, limitations, and consequences are recorded.
- The next decision is explicit: stop, adapt, repeat, propose, or scale through an accountable owner.
- A reusable lesson has been shared when it would benefit others and is safe to share.

## Interview and discovery questions

1. Which recurring part of your role consumes effort without using your distinctive judgment?
2. What would make an AI-assisted result trustworthy enough for this consequence?
3. Which information are you authorized to use, and what remains unclear?
4. How would you know that a polished answer is fundamentally wrong?
5. Where does your faster output create new review or cleanup work for someone else?
6. What safe failure could you practice before attempting the real task?
7. Who can challenge or approve the action if it crosses your authority?
8. What could you teach a peer from your latest successful or failed experiment?

## Connections to the 15 operating rules

This persona directly embodies **Make Teaching and Learning Part of Everyone's Job** and **Stay Flexible in Pursuit of Value**. It supports **Put Product Managers in the Working Environment Every Day**, **Turn Complaints Into Fixes or Complete Proposals**, **Treat Documentation as Executable Infrastructure**, **Build With a Coordinated Team, Not Alone**, and **Assume Best Intent**. The Learner makes transformation participatory: people do not merely receive new tools; they build, test, critique, document, and spread better ways of working.
