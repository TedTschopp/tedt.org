## Objective

Create a comprehensive reference model showing how a large, regulated enterprise responds when an employee encounters a technology problem or identifies a technology-related need.

Begin with an employee experiencing a problem. Follow the work from initial recognition and contact through diagnosis, restoration, root-cause analysis, permanent correction, product improvement, organizational learning, measurement of outcomes, and eventual product or service retirement.

The result must go substantially beyond the traditional Tier 1, Tier 2, and Tier 3 support model. It must represent the full IT organization and all supporting business, governance, risk, management, supplier, and executive roles that could legitimately become involved.

The deliverable must include:

* An explanation of support tiers
* A complete role-family model
* An exhaustive catalog of individual roles
* Multiple end-to-end user journeys
* A complex capstone journey involving nearly the entire organization
* Organizational handoffs and accountabilities
* Role descriptions

## Important Modeling Rules

Follow these rules throughout the deliverable.

### Distinguish roles from other concepts

Clearly distinguish:

* Role
* Job title
* Position
* Responsibility
* Team
* Department
* Practice
* Process
* Committee
* Forum
* Tool
* Technology
* Vendor
* Organizational capability

For example, a Change Advisory Board is a forum, not necessarily a job role. Incident management is a practice, while an Incident Manager is a role. Product ownership may be a dedicated job or a responsibility assigned to another job.

### Do not force every role into every journey

Different types of work involve different roles.

Create separate journeys and show which roles are:

* Always involved
* Usually involved
* Conditionally involved
* Consulted
* Informed
* Not normally involved

Do not invent unnecessary handoffs merely to include a role.

To address the need to understand the entire organization, create:

1. A complete organizational interaction map showing all role families
2. A series of realistic journeys involving the appropriate roles
3. A capstone scenario in which nearly every role family has a legitimate reason to participate
4. An explanation of why some individual roles may still not participate in the capstone scenario

### Preserve end-to-end accountability

For each journey, identify separately:

* The person accountable for the employee experience
* The person accountable for service restoration
* The person accountable for incident coordination
* The person accountable for technical diagnosis
* The person accountable for the product

* The person accountable for funding

## Required User Journeys

Create separate end-to-end journeys for at least the following scenarios.

### Journey 1: Standard service request

Examples include:

* Requesting approved software
* Requesting a device
* Requesting standard access
* Requesting a collaboration resource

### Journey 2: Routine employee incident

An employee cannot perform a normal work activity because a technology component is not functioning.

### Journey 3: Complex cross-technology incident

The symptom appears in one product, but the underlying cause involves multiple applications, integrations, identity systems, infrastructure components, or external providers.

### Journey 4: Major production incident

A high-impact service interruption requires coordinated technical response, business communication, leadership visibility, and formal recovery.

### Journey 5: Recurring defect and problem management

A repeatedly restored incident requires root-cause analysis, engineering remediation, testing, deployment, and verification.

### Journey 6: Product enhancement

A user’s reported problem is not a defect but reveals an unmet need or product-design limitation.

### Journey 7: New digital product or business capability

The employee or business identifies a need that cannot reasonably be addressed through support or a small enhancement.

### Journey 8: Cybersecurity event

A user report, automated control, monitoring system, vendor notification, or threat-intelligence source identifies a potential cybersecurity event.

### Journey 9: Proactively detected reliability, capacity, or performance issue

Monitoring identifies degradation before an employee reports it.

### Journey 10: Regulatory, audit, privacy, or control finding

A control failure or compliance obligation requires technology and process change.

### Journey 11: Technical debt remediation

Engineering, security, architecture, operations, or product management identifies technical debt that creates unacceptable risk, cost, or delivery friction.

### Journey 12: Product or service retirement

A product or service progresses from active use through deprecation, migration, retirement, records retention, contract termination, and disposal.

## Primary Employee-Problem Journey

Give special attention to a detailed primary journey that begins as follows:

> An employee attempts to perform a business task, encounters a technology problem, and seeks assistance.

Walk through the full chain, including where appropriate:

1. Employee recognizes the problem.
2. Employee uses self-service or contacts support.
3. Request or incident is recorded.
4. Identity and entitlement are confirmed.
5. Initial diagnosis occurs.
6. Existing knowledge is searched.
7. Resolution is attempted.
8. Work is escalated or reassigned.
9. Product and service ownership are identified.
10. Cross-system dependencies are analyzed.
11. Incident coordination is established.
12. Business impact is assessed.
13. Cybersecurity implications are evaluated.
14. Temporary restoration is performed.
15. Root-cause analysis begins.
16. Problem records or engineering work items are created.
17. Product management determines whether the issue is a defect, enhancement, operational issue, training issue, process issue, vendor issue, or architectural issue.
18. Funding and prioritization decisions are made.
19. Architecture and control implications are evaluated.
20. The solution is designed.
21. Software, configuration, infrastructure, data, integration, security, or vendor changes are implemented.
22. Testing and validation occur.
23. Change and release controls are satisfied.
24. The solution is deployed.
25. Service restoration and permanent remediation are verified separately.
26. The employee and affected business stakeholders are informed.
27. Knowledge and support procedures are updated.
28. Monitoring and controls are improved.
29. Product metrics and business outcomes are measured.
30. Lessons are incorporated into future work.
31. The capability is maintained over time.
32. The product or service is eventually retired.

## Dreyfus Skill Model

Use the following five levels:

1. Novice
2. Advanced Beginner
3. Competent
4. Proficient
5. Expert

Do not assign one Dreyfus level to a person or role as a whole. Apply the model separately to each skill.

A person may simultaneously be:

* Expert in one technical domain
* Proficient in incident diagnosis
* Competent in stakeholder communication
* An Advanced Beginner in product management

Do not equate Dreyfus Expert with job title, tenure, management level, or organizational grade.

* How the person interprets context

* How much supervision is needed
* What complexity the person can handle
* How the person prioritizes
* How the person responds to ambiguity
* How the person uses judgment

Use concrete behavioral descriptions.

For example:

* **Novice:** Follows explicit diagnostic procedures and escalates when conditions differ from the procedure.
* **Advanced Beginner:** Recognizes recurring symptoms and applies prior examples but needs help prioritizing unfamiliar signals.
* **Competent:** Independently creates a diagnostic plan, prioritizes competing hypotheses, and coordinates routine cross-team investigation.
* **Proficient:** Recognizes system-level patterns, anticipates likely failure modes, and adapts the investigation based on context.
* **Expert:** Identifies novel or weakly signaled failure modes, develops new diagnostic approaches, teaches others, and changes organizational practice.
