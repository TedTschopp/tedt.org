But these handsome knightly seats were all devastated and broken in the unrest of war that erupted in 1386 between the people of Lucerne and Hemmann von Grünenberg over various customs and border disputes. When Reichensee and Baldegg had already been consumed by flames, the enemy wished to prepare the same fate for the last remaining castle, Heidegg. At that time a particularly devout and charitable noblewoman named Bertha lived there. She was already a widow and had only one child. Trembling for the child’s life as the enemy approached, she vowed to found a chapel to the Mother of God. After the warriors destroyed Lieli Castle, they advanced through the dense forest beneath the Klotisberg and stormed toward the hill upon which Heidegg stands. At once, however, the whole region was shrouded in such impenetrable fog, and the Dünkelbach flowing down by the castle began to swell and roar so violently, that in the darkness and the noise of the water the enemy strayed from the path, plunged into the raging stream, and was swept in its flood toward Lake Baldegg. Those who escaped were able to find their bearings again only on the ridge of the Lindenberg, on the upper Illnau. Thus the castle was spared. In due gratitude, the lady of the castle then built the chapel that still stands beside the castle and bears the inscription: “Procul estote profani!
