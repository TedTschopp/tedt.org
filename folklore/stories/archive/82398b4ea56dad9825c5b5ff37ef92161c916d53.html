A boy was tending sheep on the Surenen Alp. It is called that because the wild stream that rises there once bore the same name; today it is generally called the Engelberger-Aa.", "At that time, the Alp belonged to the people of Engelberg. Whenever the boy ran short of food, he slaughtered a sheep, and when he had gathered several hides, he carried them by night to “Urscheln” (Ursern) to trade for cheese and Ziger. He had just done so when a flock of lambs and sheep arrived from Wälschland. They pleased him tremendously, far more than his own. In his entire flock, he possessed none like them and had never seen their kind before. He began to beg and plead insistently for a young lamb. They pointed out that he had no money to pay for it and was only a beggar boy. But the Surenen shepherd did not give up and continued begging. At last, they promised him the little lamb if he would kneel and pray a rosary. He answered that his mother had taught him the prayer but that he had practiced it little; then he fulfilled the condition and received his reward. Rejoicing, the boy returned over the Surenenecke to the Alp. His love for the little animal he had acquired was beyond all measure. It had to eat and sleep with him and always be near him. At last he thought that it should be baptized too, since he himself had been baptized. He therefore went down over the Surenenecke to the church in Attinghausen, where he broke open the baptismal font and took baptismal water. Having returned home by the same path, he baptized the lamb according to the Christian faith. Oh, if only he had not committed that sacrilege against God's will! Scarcely had it happened when a dreadful storm roared through the air. The beloved, dainty lamb changed into a terrible monster. In a horrifying tempest, it at once smashed its master's hut to pieces; then it rushed upon the shepherd boy and avenged the desecration of the sacrament in his blood. The specter no longer spared or tolerated either people or livestock on Surenen. From then on, the people called the dreadful fiend the Greiss. The people of Engelberg grew weary of the Alp and sold it cheaply to the people of Uri for a quarter Bemsch—that is to say, a Zweischilliger. It fared no better for the people of Uri, who got as bad a bargain as the former owners. One day, when the sagacious council of Uri was assembled at the Löwen inn—which, incidentally, is said to be the oldest in the village—and its members were telling the story of Surenen, a strange little man listened. He soon joined the discussion and said he could help them if they filled his little cup with wine twice. They gladly quenched his thirst. The little man then advised them to let a silver-white bull calf suckle for seven years, each year from one more cow than the year before, until there were seven cows and the bull calf was seven years old. It would then be capable of killing the Greiss. Now they had trouble finding such a calf. At last they found one belonging to a man from the Schächental, whom they wished to pay well for it, but he asked nothing in return. So well fed, the young animal soon grew astonishingly strong and large. When it was four years old, no one could any longer be near it or handle it because of its wildness and unruliness. They therefore moved the bull to the Waldnacht Alp, toward Surenen. Even now, people point out the Stierengaden there, where the seven years were completed. According to the wise little man's counsel, a pure maiden, the noblest in the land, was now to lead the bull from there to meet the Greiss. Again they were in difficulty until the right maiden was found in Attinghausen. She was willing to attempt it, first purified herself at the convent in Seedorf, and prepared for death. A great procession of people went from the church at Attinghausen with the maiden, who was dressed in white, as far as the Stierengaden. Here the pure maiden had to tie the wild bull to her hair ribbons and then guide it over the ridge toward Surenen. Untamed though it otherwise was, it submitted without resistance. Following the little man's instructions, the maiden was further told that when the bull came near the Greiss, it would scent the monster and give her an unmistakable sign, whereupon it was to be untied. Once that was done, she was to make her way back at a run and under no circumstances look around, no matter what she might hear. Everything happened in this manner except for the final instruction. From the required distance, the people watched in the greatest suspense toward the place where they supposed the battle to be and waited for its outcome. Terrible bellowing was heard, and a column of smoke that darkened the sun rose into the sky; then the maiden's white garments were seen flying about a rock. There followed a deep silence as the smoke vanished. Then they said, “Now the battle is over; let us go and see what has happened.” They saw nothing more of the maiden. The Greiss, badly mauled, had been killed. The victorious giant bull likewise lay dead in the Alpine stream, probably because after the heat of battle it had drunk too greedily from it. For that reason, the water was called Stierenbach. On a rock, people point out the hoofprints it struck there during the fight. The region had been freed from the Greiss—but not entirely. Even now, when young cattle on the Alp often suddenly fall dead, the herders say that the Greiss has struck them. Every year, a related supplicatory procession still goes to the chapel on the Surenenalp, which is admittedly very distant from Attinghausen.
