He quickened his steps to offer her his services in case she had lost her way. As he drew near, he noticed that she was beautiful, young, and noble. What struck him most, however, was that she wore no covering upon her head and walked barefoot. Rain dripped from her magnificent hair, which fell in rich curls upon her shoulders. A gold chain hung upon her lily-white neck, a costly girdle encircled her slender waist, and her arms were likewise adorned with golden Brasseln. Rings set with diamonds gleamed upon the fingers of her small, snow-white hands. Her bare feet, reddened by cold and wet, seemed so delicate that every little stone must have wounded them. With one hand she modestly held up her silk apron to ease her passage through the rough country; in the other she carried a long walking staff. She stepped so cautiously with her delicate feet upon the hard, cold, wet stones that one could see every step was difficult and caused her pain. Her lovely face bore traces of much weeping, fresh tears still shimmered in her large, gentle eyes, and her fine lips opened in quiet sighs and prayers. Full of wonder at this strange apparition and moved by deep compassion, he asked, “But for God's sake, my beautiful, good lady, where are you going in such harsh weather and such a wild region? You must be completely lost! Ah, may God have mercy! You are walking barefoot, without a hat or umbrella; surely some misfortune has befallen you? Or where are your servants? Did you take no guide? Surely you did not come all the way here on foot? No doubt you dismounted not far from here and have wandered too far from your companions and lost your way?” “No, my good young man,” the lady answered in a lovely voice, “I have not lost my way. I truly come here without companions, without horse, without servants, without hat, shoes, or umbrella. I have just come from a great city and splendid palace. My body still lies warm upon its deathbed in Milan, around which my beloved parents bitterly weep for their only daughter and wet it with their tears. God has condemned me to atone in this glacier because during my life I scarcely ever trod upon earth, because I always rode in a carriage, never entered a Traufe, never left the house without stately attendants, never exposed myself to a cold little breeze, was never permitted to deny myself any respectable pleasure, and feared every exertion and hardship. Therefore, as punishment for my pampered existence, I am condemned to walk barefoot through this rough wilderness in rain, cold, and storm and to atone in this glacier—this is my Fegfeuer—for apart from this pampering I committed no sin.” At these final words, a dense, dark fog and a cold rain shower suddenly swept in and took the lovely figure from his sight. When, after a few moments, the rain shower and thick fog had passed and the region brightened somewhat again, no trace of the beautiful woman could be seen. At once, but unfortunately too late, it occurred to him that God had not permitted her to appear to him in such a beautiful form without reason. Surely only a little had been lacking for her complete redemption. Ah, instead of asking useless questions, he should have offered his help so that he might redeem her. As loudly as he could, he now called into the region where she had vanished, “Beautiful lady, tell me, what can I do to redeem you?” But instead of an answer, each time only a faint echo of his last words returned. The brook murmured melancholically; the glacier thundered dully; pale shapes of fog rose and fell from the glacial crevasses—but he saw and heard nothing more of her. Whenever later a wondrous longing led him back to this wild region in fog and rain, he sat in the same place where the noble lady's delicate feet had stood, turned his face toward the region where she had vanished, dreamed the former lovely apparition vividly back into being, and often called in a loud voice, “Beautiful lady, can I still do anything to redeem you?” The same faint echo always returned from the rocks as before. Often thick, dark fogs with cold showers passed him as they had then; the valley brook murmured just as melancholically, and the glacier sounded the same dull thunder as before.
