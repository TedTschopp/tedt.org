The country people of Zug, Walchwil, and Ägeri tell the following about it:", "In very ancient times, Walchwil formed a small community. A rich woman from Schwyz gave it the common pasture on the Zugerberg. Because this common land was so extensive—more than sufficient for the needs of the people of Walchwil—neighbors from the city municipality of Zug were also granted permission to drive cattle onto it. In time, the people of Zug turned this freely granted permission into a right. A serious dispute arose between the two communities over possession of a large part of the Walchwil common pasture. At an open court held upon the disputed ground, a man from Zug, the Bannwart, appeared riding a white horse and served as both witness and judge. Cunningly, before leaving home he had put soil from his garden into his shoes and hidden beneath his hat a spoon called a Schöpfer and a comb called a Richt-Strähl. When questioned under oath before the court, he declared: ‘As surely as I have the Creator and the Judge above me, so surely do I stand upon the soil and ground of Zug.’ As a result of this false oath, judgment went against the people of Walchwil. Soon after that day, perhaps while returning home from the inspection court, the false witness met with an accident—and died.", "Later he appeared under the name Bannhölzler as a malicious mountain spirit who inflicted all kinds of harm upon the cattle of Walchwil. A spirit-binder from the Entlebuch named Krummenacher bound him into the rock face on the Rossberg, where three round holes can still be seen today. The Bannhölzler himself was confined in the middle hole, from which red water flows; his white horse went into the hole on the left, marked by blue water; and his faithful dog into the one on the right, from which yellow water flowed. Here the spirit caused many an incautious person to plunge over his rocky grave after luring them with the sight of lovely flowers. If called out from the common pasture, he instantly appeared in a terrible form. He could not cross beyond the Marchen of the pasture. Mischievous herder boys sometimes called him forth while keeping ready to leap across the boundary line when he came. Once a goat boy cried: ‘Bannhölzler! Come—if you catch me, you may have me!’ He then quickly slipped beneath the boundary hedge. He scarcely succeeded, for the spirit tore a wooden shoe from his foot.", "On the Walchwil mountain stood a house whose site is still pointed out today. Beside the house was a skittle ground. The terrified young people fled; a girl slipped backward beneath the fence, and the Bannhölzler tore both braids from her head. Then he took the ball and hurled it against the house with such force that it flew through both walls and out the opposite side.
