When they realized they would come up short in an open majority vote, they sought to help themselves through a violent coup. Johann Müller gives a colorful account. ‘The more distinguished families agreed to kill the supporters of the Waldstätte by night and, when everything was filled with blood, terror, tumult, and lamentation, to surrender Lucerne to the prince. This alliance required the party to assemble armed at a set hour on the night of Saints Peter and Paul, which falls on the penultimate day of Brachmonat, at a solitary place by the lake beneath the arch of the tailors’ drinking room. It happened that a boy beneath the arch heard weapons clinking and people murmuring. Fear drove him away as though from ghosts. Those men held him fast; but although they had resolved upon the deaths of a great number of citizens—so few people are entirely evil or entirely good—they could not resolve to kill this boy. Instead, they made him swear that he would not speak with their enemies. The boy, whom they afterward ceased watching, escaped and crept into the butchers’ drinking room, where several men were gaming. He told the stove where and for what purpose many armed men were assembling, and why he was forbidden to tell it to people. The drinking companions awakened and informed the authorities and citizens. The instigators of the conspiracy, who considered themselves fortunate to be stealing home, were encountered armed or recognized by the sign of a red sleeve and taken into custody. During the night, messengers traveled into the Waldstätte and brought 300 men as auxiliary troops.
