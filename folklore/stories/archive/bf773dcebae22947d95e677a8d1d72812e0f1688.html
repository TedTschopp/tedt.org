As though that were not enough, a town brook of its own also runs lengthwise through it, and despite the existing abundance of water power, no good local citizen will ever allow the indispensability of this very brook to be disputed. In earlier times the town emphatically named itself after this modest brook as ‘Aarau situated on the Susbach.’ Whether it was called Susbach after the municipal trading house it passes, which a charter of liberties issued by Duke Leopold III of Austria in the year 1381 already calls the Sustenhaus (warehouse), or whether the generous Susanna Schännis lent it her baptismal name when she presented this town brook to the community, is not at issue here. The special esteem accorded the little brook is an established fact and continues to this day. The following tradition is told about it.", "In the year 1270, the mayor and council of Aarau accepted into citizenship, protection, and shelter the community of consecrated Sisters of Christ vowed to God, called those of Schännis, and gave them a homestead between the walls and the Aare River (Aargau Contributions by Kurz and Weissenbach, 1847, p. 547).", "A fire had deprived them of their women's religious foundation at Schännis in the Gaster region—today a parish village of St. Gallen—and left them homeless. Because they were also exempted from all municipal assessments and Tellen, their gratitude was doubled. A noblewoman of the Schännis foundation therefore offered the town a service in return. She allowed the citizens to choose whether they wished her to have made for them a golden chain that would encircle their walls, or instead to find them a spring as powerful as a brook and have it channeled through the whole settlement. The citizens' concern for the common good and the welfare of their descendants made them choose the latter, rejecting the glittering wealth of gold.", "And so, at a distance of a quarter hour from the town, abundant mountain springs must gather near the village of Sur and, after only a few minutes, flow toward the town as a calm brook filled to its banks. The manuscript town chronicle of U. Fisch states under the year 1688: on the 4th day of the autumn month, the citizens of Aarau marched out with drums and fifes, according to ancient custom and liberty, to fish the brook. This veneration may indicate an old religious relationship between the inhabitants and a body of water that is tame and benevolent and does not take a human life in sacrifice like the wild Aare River. Every year in high summer, after the brook has been shut off for cleaning and then released again, a general holiday begins. The schools are closed, and factories and workshops stand idle. Young and old, dressed in their Sunday best and adorned with Maien and switches, march to the beat of drums upstream to the brook's source, then in the evening march back through the gate in military order with the returning waters.
