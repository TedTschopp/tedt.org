After he had lived there for a time, he had a pole erected beneath the linden tree where everyone had to pass, placed a hat upon it, and stationed a servant beside it as guard. He then commanded by public proclamation that whoever passed there should bow to the hat as if the lord himself were present; if anyone overlooked it and failed to do so, he would punish that person with heavy fines. Now, there was a pious man in the country named William Tell. He passed before the hat and did not bow to it even once. The servant who guarded the hat therefore denounced him to the Landvogt. The Landvogt had Tell brought before him and asked why he did not bow to the pole and hat, although it had been commanded. William Tell answered: ‘Dear lord, it happened by chance; I did not think Your Grace would regard and take it so seriously. If I were clever, I would be called something other than Tell.’ Tell was an excellent marksman, such as no other could be found in the country, and he also had fine children whom he loved. The Landvogt sent for the children, and when they came, he asked Tell which child was dearest to him. ‘They are all equally dear to me.’", "Then the lord said: ‘William, you are a good marksman, and your equal cannot be found; you shall prove that to me now, for you must shoot an apple from the head of one of your children. If you do that, I shall regard you as a good marksman.’ Good Tell was terrified and pleaded for mercy and to be excused from such a thing, for it was unnatural; whatever else the vogt ordered, he would gladly do. But the vogt compelled him with his servants and himself placed the apple upon the child's head. Tell now saw that he could not evade it, took an arrow and stuck it behind him into his Göller, took the other arrow in his hand, spanned the crossbow, and prayed to God to protect his child. He aimed and successfully shot the apple from the child's head without harm. Then the lord said it was a master shot. ‘But you shall tell me one thing: what did it mean that you thrust the first arrow behind you into your Göller?’", "Tell said: ‘That is the custom of marksmen.’ But the Landvogt would not desist and wished to hear the true reason. At last, Tell, who feared to reveal the truth, said that if the vogt guaranteed his life, he would tell it. When the Landvogt had done so, Tell said: ‘Very well! Since you have guaranteed my life, I shall tell the truth.’ He began and said: ‘I did it for this reason: had I missed the apple and shot my little child, I would not have missed you with the other arrow.’", "When the Landvogt heard this, he said: ‘Your life has indeed been promised to you, but I shall put you in a place where neither sun nor moon will ever shine upon you.’ He had Tell captured and bound and placed in the same boat in which he intended to sail back to Schwyz. As they traveled on the lake and came out as far as Axen, a cruel, powerful wind struck them so that the vessel pitched, and they believed they would miserably perish, for no one any longer knew how to steer the craft through the waves. Then, one of the servants said to the Landvogt: ‘Lord, order Tell unbound. He is a strong, powerful man and understands the weather well; then we might escape this peril.’ The lord spoke and called to Tell: ‘If you will help us and do your utmost to get us away from here, I shall order you unbound.’ Tell said: ‘Yes, gracious lord, I shall gladly do it and trust myself capable.’ Tell was unbound, stood at the helm, and sailed skillfully onward; yet all the while, he looked everywhere for his advantage and toward his crossbow, which lay on the floor near him. When he came opposite a great rock slab—ever since called the Tellen Platte, as it is still called today—it seemed to him time to escape. He called cheerfully to everyone to pull hard until they reached the slab, for if they passed it, they would have overcome the worst. Thus, they drew near the slab. Then, with force, for he was an exceedingly strong man, he swung the boat, seized his crossbow, and leaped onto the slab. He pushed the vessel away from himself and left it drifting and pitching on the lake. He ran through Schwyz schattenhalb—along the shadowed, dark mountains—until he reached Küssnacht in the hohle Gassen. There, he had arrived before the lord and waited for him. When the Landvogt rode up with his servants, Tell stood behind a bush and heard all kinds of plans being made against him. He spanned the crossbow and shot an arrow into the lord so that he fell dead.
