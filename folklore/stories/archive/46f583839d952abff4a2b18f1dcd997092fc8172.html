Silver mines had been discovered at the summit of the Rothhorn above Churwalden, so productive that day and night trains of mules were on the road carrying ore to the smelter.* There are even said to have been little streams of liquid gold from which a Masskanne of the purest metal could be drawn each morning and evening. Amid this great wealth, the simplicity of Alpine pastoral life soon disappeared entirely, and pride and the coarsest hard-heartedness took hold. Everything that was not gold and did not bring in gold served the people only for mockery. They were ashamed to remember that they descended from poor mountain shepherds and that, within living memory, they had still gone about in the same coarse woolen coat as every other Senne in the neighboring Alpine huts. Such was the state of Plurs in the year 1618, just as a wedding of unprecedented splendor was to be celebrated. Beneath canopies embroidered with gold, the company had gone to church; they had dined from silver plate and now strolled after the meal along the banks of the Maira, where an unexpected new festivity was meant to surprise the bridal couple. The refined company even considered the idea witty and clever: a stray animal that they could have seized and killed on their own estate would be sent home to its owner alive, only without its fleece. At last the outrage was done, and the animal’s hide had been stripped away. But such cruelty toward poor animals and poor people was not to go unpunished; the arrogant town was ripe for destruction.
