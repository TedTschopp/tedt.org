A Workforce Splitting in Two — and It Is a Management Problem

A survey from Lenny's Newsletter examines how generative AI is reshaping the technology workforce, and the headline finding is less about capability than culture: organizations are developing two distinct internal groups in parallel — employees who feel empowered and productive with AI, and those experiencing anxiety, burnout, and genuine fear of displacement.

Why it matters: The divide is not simply a training problem. It reflects deeper organizational dynamics around psychological safety, role clarity, and leadership communication. Left unmanaged, it becomes a talent retention and performance risk, particularly as AI adoption accelerates in the second half of 2026. Enterprises that treat AI transformation as a technology deployment rather than a people program are likely to see the anxious cohort either disengage or leave.

What should I do as a leader? Audit workforce AI sentiment across your teams — not just adoption rates. Identify where anxiety is concentrated and build targeted enablement programs. Clear messaging on what AI augments, rather than what it replaces, is not optional at this stage.

What should I do? If AI adoption feels overwhelming, pursue structured training proactively. Early adopters in this survey reported the largest productivity gains, but they also had access to deliberate onboarding — not just tool access.

The Real Competitive Edge Is the Layer Around the Model

Lilian Weng — former VP of Research at OpenAI — published a detailed essay arguing that harness engineering, the orchestration layer that wraps and coordinates large language models through workflows, memory, sub-agents, and evaluation loops, has become the primary mechanism for building self-improving AI systems. The foundation model itself is increasingly commoditized; what differentiates enterprise AI capability is the infrastructure built around it.

Why it matters: This is a strategic reframe with direct procurement and investment implications. Enterprises that have spent 2025 acquiring model access without investing in evaluation and orchestration infrastructure are renting capability rather than building it. The organizations that will compound advantage over the next 18 months are those that treat orchestration architecture as a core engineering discipline, not a vendor-optional feature.

What should I do as a leader? Shift at least a portion of AI investment away from model access licenses and toward orchestration infrastructure, evaluation systems, and the engineering teams who can build and sustain them. Ask your AI program leads how your current systems improve themselves over time — and be direct about expecting an answer.

DoorDash's Practical Blueprint for Trusting AI in Code Review

DoorDash published a detailed case study on how it built DashBench — an internal AI evaluation framework that uses historical pull requests to benchmark AI code reviewers across multiple quality metrics and model combinations. Rather than relying on vendor benchmarks or team intuition, DoorDash created measurement infrastructure to answer a specific operational question: is this AI reviewer actually trustworthy in our codebase?

Why it matters: The DashBench approach — real historical test cases, multi-model comparison, continuous benchmarking — is directly replicable for any enterprise deploying AI in engineering, audit, legal review, or quality assurance workflows. The insight driving it applies broadly: AI trust is not a feeling, it is a measurement problem. Enterprises that skip this step are accumulating unmanaged risk alongside their productivity gains.

What should I do as a leader? Before expanding AI code review or AI-assisted quality processes, invest in evaluation infrastructure. A tool without a measurement framework is a tool you cannot manage, govern, or defend in an audit.

What should I do? If your team is adopting AI code review, help establish baseline quality metrics now. Waiting until deployment matures makes it far harder to detect when performance drifts.

Anthropic Finds Evidence for How LLMs Actually Reason

Anthropic published research introducing evidence for what it calls a "global workspace" — referred to as J-space — inside large language models: an internal representational space that appears to support planning, multi-step reasoning, and more controllable model behavior. The findings come from interpretability research, and Anthropic also released an interactive presentation to make the concepts accessible to non-researchers.

Why it matters: Interpretability has moved from a theoretical aspiration to a vendor evaluation criterion. For enterprises, understanding whether and how a model reasons — rather than just pattern-matches — has direct implications for deploying AI in workflows where errors carry compliance, financial, or reputational consequences. Research like this makes it possible to ask more specific questions of AI vendors about hallucination reduction and reasoning reliability, rather than accepting blanket safety claims.

What should I do as a leader? Add reasoning transparency and interpretability evidence to your AI vendor evaluation framework. Ask vendors specifically how their models handle multi-step reasoning and uncertainty — not just whether they have an AI safety team.
